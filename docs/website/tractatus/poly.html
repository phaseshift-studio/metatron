    The poly types are one of the more interesting aspects of metatron. They are used to structure data (serving as collections) and for flow control (code branching). There are 3 poly types in metatron: rec (record), lst (list), and rel (relation) — the linchpins of metatron.

    data structures

    The obvious application of a poly is as the scaffolding for a data structure. A poly is a collection able to bundle multiple objs together. Via the colocation of two objs within the confines of a poly, two objs are bound. Another way to bind two objs together is via explicit uri referencing. This method is a form of lazy binding in which "colocation" is computed through dereferencing, and as such, dangling pointers are possible. With poly, there can be no "dangling pointers" — either two objs are colocated in the poly or not. However, mtron is lenient on lazy pointers. It is important to realize the ramifications of the fail-free aspect of noobj resolved pointers.

    graceful degradation in the void with noobj

    The intended use of metatron is to connect disparate datasets and processors into a cohesive structure bound by loose structural similarities. From resources on the web, to LLMs on networked GPU, to vertices linked within a graph database, data entropy will come to fragment structural bonds as different stakeholders within the larger data infrastructure independently alter their sphere of "data influence." In uncertain data environments, noobj and it’s manifestation in maybe/maybe_some coefficients {?}/{*} make dangling pointers and type assumptions convenient for the user.

    mtron> 1-<[?>0,?>2,*/usr/${_}]     ​1​
==>[1,noobj,noobj]
mtron> 1-<[?>0,?>2,*/usr/${_}]>-     ​2​
==>1
mtron> 1-<[?>0,?>2,*/usr/${_}]>-.map(<${g:V/_}>)>>+     ​3​
    1 dangling pointers that lead to nowhere, lead to noobj.
    2 mtron silently ignores noobj and continues with those objs it knows.
    3 address wildcards (+) are a foothold in a noobj-world.

    Relations

    mtron> a=>b=>c     ​1​
==>a=>(b=>c)
mtron> [a=>{b,c},
        b=>{a,c},
        c=>{a,b}]     ​2​
==>[a=>{b,c},b=>{a,c},c=>{a,b}]
mtron> [a,b,c]     ​3​
==>[a,b,c]
    1 a rel combines two objs, but due to associative nature of the => operator, a rel can bind an obj to another rel.
    2 a rec can be understood as a "bag of `rels`", but this conceptualization looses its hold when considering control and query.
    3 a lst combines objs via positional association.

    rel/traverse vs. to/from

    Consider the following expressions:

    mtron> a=>b=>c     ​1​
==>a=>(b=>c)
mtron> a->b->c     ​2​
==>c
    1 a rel associates a to b=>c and another rel associates b to c.
    2 to?uri<=uri associates a to b and another to?uri<=uri associates b to c. 
    mtron> a=>b=>c.>>     ​1​
==>b=>c
mtron> a=>b=>c.>>.>>     ​2​
==>c
mtron> a->b->c.*a     ​3​
==>b
mtron> a->b->c.**a     ​4​
==>c
    1 >>?#<=rel maps a rel to it’s rng.
    2 Two >> yields the tail of the relational chain, c.
    3 *?#<=uri maps a uri to an obj.
    4 Two ** yields the tail of the referential chain, c.

    The third expression constructs and traverses its data structure on the stack. The fourth expression constructs and traverses its data structure in space. It just so happens that the space that the fourth expression is operating in is the stack. This is due to the fact that <+/#> is the stack’s pattern, and therefore, matches a, b, c.

    mtron> [== a->(b->c) ==]     ​1​
mtron> *a
==>b
mtron> **a
==>c
    1 a references the instruction b→c.
    2 *a resolves to the instruction b→c which, as an auto (!), immediately applies a to b yielding c.

    Control Structures

    Most languages choose a path with keywords: if/else, switch/case, match, cond. Each is a construct of its own, with its own rules for what is tested, in what order, and what happens when nothing matches. In metatron, choosing is done with data. A rec is a relation, and the rec algebra already holds every one of those constructs. ` gathers alternatives into a table. `*` composes relations: each path of the left grows at its last element by every pair of the right whose key admits that element. Composition distributes over `, so composing with a table is composing with each of its alternatives and gathering what results.

    \(R + S = R \cup S \qquad R * S = \{\, p \Rightarrow c \mid p \in R,\ \mathrm{last}(p) \Rightarrow c \in S \,\} \qquad R * (S + T) = R * S + R * T\)

    mtron> a=>b=>c=>d     ​1​
==>a=>(b=>(c=>d))
mtron> [a=>b] + [b=>c] + [c=>a] + [c=>d] + [d=>b]     ​2​
==>[a=>b,b=>c,c=>{a,d},d=>b]
mtron> [a=>b] * [b=>c]     ​3​
==>[a=>b=>c]
    1 => associates to the right, so a chain of relations is a path.
    2 + unions relations, and pairs that share a key bulk under it.
    3 * grows the path a=>b at b by b=>c.

    To select with this algebra, an obj is made the end of a path, and a table of alternatives is composed onto it. A table’s pairs read key => location => code: the key says which objs the alternative admits, the location where its code runs, and the code what runs. Here the locations and codes are names; a location may be a machine and a code a program.

    mtron> 3.-<[|# => _]     ​1​
==>[<#>=>3]
mtron> 1.plus(2).-<[|# => _] * [1 => a => codeA,
                                2 => b => codeB,
                                3 => c => codeC]     ​2​
==>[<#>=>3=>c=>codeC]
mtron> 1.plus(2).-<[|# => _] * [1 => a => codeA,
                                2 => b => codeB,
                                3 => c => codeC].{3}>>     ​3​
==>codeC
    1 splitting (-<) an obj into a rec lifts it into a relation. The key | is the pattern (any obj), blocked so it stays as written, and _ is the obj itself.
    2 composition matches 3 against the table’s keys, and the path grows by the alternative 3 names: a location, c, and a code, codeC.
    3 three steps (.{3}>>) walk the path to its code.

    guards, the first match, and the default

    A key need not be a value. A predicate is a key that admits every obj it holds for, and a type is a key that admits its instances. Composition takes every key that admits the obj, so a table of guards sends an obj down every guard it passes. Projecting each path onto its first, ==[_ => take(1)], keeps the first guard in written order.

    mtron> 1.plus(1).-<[|# => _] * [?>0 => a => codeA, ?>1 => b => codeB, ?>2 => c => codeC].{3}>>     ​1​
==>codeA
==>codeB
mtron> 1.plus(1).-<[|# => _] * [?>0 => a => codeA, ?>1 => b => codeB, ?>2 => c => codeC]==[_ => take(1)].{3}>>     ​2​
==>codeA
mtron> 5.-<[|# => _] * [int::T => a => codeA, str::T => b => codeB].{3}>>     ​3​
==>codeA
mtron> 'five'.-<[|# => _] * [int::T => a => codeA, str::T => b => codeB].{3}>>     ​4​
==>codeB
    1 2 passes ?>0 and ?>1: two paths, two codes.
    2 the first path alone, an if/else if chain.
    3 a type admits its instances: 5 is an int …​
    4 …​ and 'five' is a str.

    The key _ admits every obj. Alone it is a fallback, and with the first match, written last, it is an else.

    mtron> 7.-<[|# => _] * [1 => a => codeA, 2 => b => codeB, _ => d => codeD].{3}>>     ​1​
==>codeD
mtron> 2.-<[|# => _] * [1 => a => codeA, 2 => b => codeB, _ => d => codeD].{3}>>     ​2​
==>codeB
==>codeD
mtron> 2.-<[|# => _] * [1 => a => codeA, 2 => b => codeB, _ => d => codeD]==[_ => take(1)].{3}>>     ​3​
==>codeB
mtron> {-3,0,5}.-<[|# => _] * [?<0 => negative, ?=0 => zero, _ => positive]==[_ => take(1)].{2}>>     ​4​
==>negative
==>zero
==>positive
    1 no key names 7, so _ takes it.
    2 _ admits 2 as well, beside the key 2.
    3 written last and projected onto the first match, _ is an else.
    4 a cond: each obj of the stream takes its own first match, two steps from its answer.

    nothing, and no match

    A relation with noobj on either side never forms: noobj is nothing, not a key. So an obj whose selection yields noobj reaches no alternative at all. A selection that must name its miss uses the sentinel none, which is a key like any other.

    mtron> [x => noobj] * [1 => a => codeA, none => d => codeD]     ​1​
==>[=>]
mtron> [x => none] * [1 => a => codeA, none => d => codeD]     ​2​
==>[x=>none=>d=>codeD]
mtron> 8.-<[|# => _] * [?>9 => a => codeA, ?>10 => b => codeB].{3}>>     ​3​
    1 the relation x=>noobj never forms, and the composition is the empty rec.
    2 none is matched like any key.
    3 no guard admits 8, and nothing is selected.

    many keys, every key

    What selects need not answer one key. An obj related to several keys reaches the alternative of each. A table whose alternatives all sit under _ sends every obj to all of them.

    mtron> [x => {2,3}] * [1 => a => codeA, 2 => b => codeB, 3 => c => codeC].{3}>>     ​1​
==>codeB
==>codeC
mtron> 5.-<[|# => _] * [_ => {a => codeA, b => codeB}].{3}>>     ​2​
==>codeA
==>codeB
    1 the obj reaches the alternatives of 2 and 3.
    2 _ admits every obj, and both alternatives sit under it: a broadcast.

    composing tables

    Because composition grows a path at its last element, tables chain. A map from keys to locations, composed with a map from locations to code, keeps where a choice runs apart from what runs, and either map can be stored and reused.

    mtron> 1.-<[|# => _] * [1 => east, 2 => west] * [east => codeA, west => codeB]     ​1​
==>[<#>=>1=>east=>codeA]
mtron> [1 => east, 2 => west]@placement     ​2​
==>[1=>east,2=>west]@placement
mtron> 2.-<[|# => _] * *placement * [east => codeA, west => codeB].{3}>>     ​3​
==>codeB
    1 1 is placed east, and east runs codeA.
    2 the placement is a value at an address.
    3 moving work means rewriting the placement, not the program.

    selection is composition

    Every construct above is one composition, \((\# \Rightarrow x) * T\), of an obj \(x\) with a table \(T = \sum_i k_i \Rightarrow c_i\). What differs is what the keys are, what the selection answers, and whether the paths are projected onto the first.

    the zero

    The first match is a projection, not a construct of its own. With \(\phi_i\) the test of the \(i\)-th key, in written order, the \(i\)-th alternative receives \(\phi_i \prod_{j<i} (1 - \phi_j)\) of the stream. So overlapping guards still route each obj exactly once.

    Read a table’s middle element as a machine, and selection is distribution: the alternatives are lanes, and their locations are the places the lanes run. metatron’s branch sends an obj down every lane, as composition keeps every path, and its partition keeps the first walk, as ==[_ => take(1)] does (Ring Theoretic Distributed Computing). The algebra above needs neither instruction: flow control is a composition of relations, and the table is a value that can be built, stored, composed and rewritten like any other.
