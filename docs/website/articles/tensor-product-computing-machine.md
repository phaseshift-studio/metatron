# Introduction

metatron evaluates a program with a swarm of *monads*. A monad holds two references: one to an object, its *data
counter*, and one to an instruction, its *program counter*. The instruction a monad points at decides where the data
counter goes next, and the structure of the code decides where the program counter goes next. A computation starts
monads, runs them through the data, and ends when every monad has either halted, with no instruction left, or died,
with no object left. Its result is the objects the halted monads hold. The machine descends from the Gremlin traversal
machine [[5]](#references), whose traversers are monads of this kind.

This article states that machine as algebra. The objects span a module $M$, the instruction positions span a module
$N$, both over a ring $R$ of point coefficients, and a monad is a basis element of the tensor product
$M \otimes_R N$. Three behaviors of the machine follow from that one choice:

- bulking is the collection of like terms;
- a dead monad is zero, because a tensor with a zero factor is zero;
- a halted monad is its object, because the end of the code contributes nothing but a single dimension.

Read through the ring of programs, the data module is a module over code. Between barriers, a computation moves
instructions across the tensor sign, one per step, and so computes the canonical isomorphism $M \otimes_F F \cong M$,
where $F$ is the code ring. Barriers, the instructions that take a whole stream at once, are where that linearity
stops, and they cut a computation into strata. One more boundary is marked where it occurs: an instruction that gives
something from nothing lies outside the module, and the machine applies it by a mechanism of its own.

The definitions follow metatron's swarm processor, `BasicSwarmComputer`. Where the text says the machine does
something, the code does it, and the *In metatron* paragraphs name where. mtron is the language in which one writes
the elements of this space: data and the programs applied to it.

# Modules and Tensor Products

The machine needs four constructions from algebra [[7,8]](#references). This section states them for a reader who has
not met them.

::: **Definition 1** (Module). Let $R$ be a commutative ring. An *$R$-module* is an abelian group $M$ with a scalar
action $R \times M \to M$ that distributes over sums in both arguments and satisfies $r(sm) = (rs)m$ and $1m = m$. A
map $f : M \to M'$ is *$R$-linear* when $f(rm + sm') = r f(m) + s f(m')$.
:::

A vector space is a module over a field. Over a ring, a scalar need not have an inverse: over the integers, a module is
just an abelian group.

::: **Definition 2** (Free module). For a set $X$, the *free module* $R[X]$ is the set of finite formal sums
$\sum_x c_x\,x$ with coefficients $c_x \in R$, added coefficientwise. The elements of $X$ are its *basis*, and a linear
map out of $R[X]$ is fixed by its values on the basis.
:::

::: **Definition 3** (Tensor product). The *tensor product* $M \otimes_R N$ of two $R$-modules is the module generated
by symbols $m \otimes n$, subject to bilinearity:
$$
\begin{equation*} (m + m') \otimes n = m \otimes n + m' \otimes n,
\qquad
m \otimes (n + n') = m \otimes n + m \otimes n',
\qquad (rm) \otimes n = r\,(m \otimes n) = m \otimes (rn).
\end{equation*}
$$
:::

Two consequences of bilinearity carry the article. A tensor with a zero factor is zero:
$0 \otimes n = (0 + 0) \otimes n = 0 \otimes n + 0 \otimes n$, so $0 \otimes n = 0$. And the tensor product of free
modules is free on the pairs of their bases, $R[X] \otimes_R R[P] \cong R[X \times P]$, with $x \otimes p$ the basis
element $(x, p)$ [[7]](#references).

::: **Definition 4** (Balanced tensor product). Let $F$ be a ring, not necessarily commutative, $M$ a *right*
$F$-module, written $m \cdot f$, and $N$ a *left* $F$-module, written $f \cdot n$. Then $M \otimes_F N$ is the abelian
group generated by symbols $m \otimes n$, additive in each factor, subject also to
$$
\begin{equation*} (m \cdot f) \otimes n = m \otimes (f \cdot n).
\end{equation*}
$$
A scalar of $F$ may move across the tensor sign.
:::

When $N$ is the ring $F$ itself, the map $m \otimes f \mapsto m \cdot f$ is an isomorphism $M \otimes_F F \cong M$
[[8]](#references). Every element $m \otimes f$ equals $(m \cdot f) \otimes 1$, the scalar moved entirely onto $M$.
That isomorphism is what the machine computes (Theorem 2).

# The Machine's Modules

::: **Definition 5** (Data module). Let $X$ be the objects of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md), among them `noobj`, the
empty stream, and let $R$ be the point coefficients of [[1,2]](#references), taken commutative here: the integers, in
metatron. The *data module* is $M = R[X]/(\mathtt{noobj})$, the free module on the objects other than `noobj`, in which
`noobj` is zero. A stream is an element of $M$: the stream $\{\{2\}a, b\}$ is $2a + b$, and the empty stream is $0$.
:::

metatron's coefficients take two forms [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md). A *point* is mass at one
address, copies of one object, and it iterates $+$: it is the scalar of this module. A *span* is extent along a run
of addresses, and it iterates $\cdot$. On a value, the span is part of the object: `2{3}` is one value, curled until
an axis lays it along three slots, and so a basis element of its own. On an instruction, the span is a power in the
code ring (Definition 11). A point may also be a range of multiplicities, as `{?}3` is. The reading of a point range
is still open in [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md), and this article takes exact points.

::: **Definition 6** (Program module). A *code* is a finite word $p = i_1\,i_2 \cdots i_k$ of instructions. Its
*positions* are $P = \{1, \ldots, k\} \cup \{\dashv\}$, where $\dashv$ is the *end* of the code. Its *successor* is
$\nu(j) = j + 1$ for $j < k$, with $\nu(k) = \dashv$ and $\nu(\dashv) = \dashv$. The *program module* is $N = R[P]$.
:::

The code's structure decides the next program counter, and $\nu$ records exactly that. The end $\dashv$ is a position
like any other: a basis element of $N$, not its zero.

::: **Definition 7** (Monad space). The *monad space* of a code $p$ is $M \otimes_R N$, free on the pairs of a nonempty
object and a position. A *monad* is a basis element $x \otimes j$, whose *data counter* is $x$ and whose *program
counter* is $j$. A *frontier* is an element of the monad space, a finite sum $\sum c\,(x \otimes j)$, and the
coefficient of a monad, its point, is its *bulk*.
:::

::: **Definition 8** (Instructions). An instruction has a *domain* and a *range*, and each carries a point that is a
range of multiplicities [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md): how many objects the instruction takes, and how
many it gives. The machine reads three kinds from the domain:

- an *initial* instruction has domain $\{0\}$: it takes nothing and gives a seed;
- a *gather* has an unbounded domain, $\{*\}$ or $\{+\}$, and is a total function $G_i : M \to M$ on a whole stream;
- an instruction whose domain takes at most one object, $\{1\}$, or $\{?\}$ for zero or one, is *per-object*: it takes
  one object at a time and is a total function $f_i : X \to M$.

A per-object instruction acts on $M$ by the linear extension of $f_i$ to the nonempty objects,
$\rho(i)\big(\sum_x c_x\,x\big) = \sum_x c_x\,f_i(x)$. It is *linear* when it gives nothing from nothing,
$f_i(\mathtt{noobj}) = 0$. One whose domain admits zero may be applied to the empty stream too, and if
$f_i(\mathtt{noobj}) \neq 0$ it is *affine*: it gives something from nothing.
:::

An instruction whose domain is finite and above one takes its stream in pages; this article leaves paging aside. An
instruction whose range is $\{0\}$ is per-object and gives nothing, $f_i(x) = 0$: it is not the end of a code.

In metatron, the machine reads `Inst.isInitial` (in `BasicSwarmComputer.resolve`), `Inst.isGather` and
`Inst.isBatching` (in `runMonads`), and `Monad.halted`. A per-object instruction is one that is neither initial nor
batching, and the machine splits a monad into one monad per object before it. The predicates `isMap`, `isFilter`,
`isFlatMap` and `isScatter` classify only a per-object instruction's form (`Inst.Form`). The end $\dashv$ is marked by
the `noobj` instruction, which the machine never applies. Another instruction of range $\{0\}$, such as `sys/close`,
is applied like any other.

# Monads and Their States

::: **Definition 9** (States). A monad $x \otimes j$ is *halted* when $j = \dashv$, *waiting* when the instruction at
$j$ is a gather, and *running* otherwise. A monad whose data counter is `noobj` is *dead*.
:::

::: **Proposition 1** (A dead monad is zero). For every position $j$, $0 \otimes j = 0$.
:::

::: *Proof.* Bilinearity (Definition 3). ◻
:::

A linear instruction leaves a dead monad dead: it gives nothing from nothing, so a sum ignores it. A filter that
rejects its object gives the empty stream, $f_i(x) = 0$, and the monad it would have made is zero.

**Live proof.** A filter keeps 2 and 3, and the monad that held 1 is gone.

```mtron
mtron> {1,2,3}.is(gt(1))
==>2
==>3
```
The machine also walks a dead monad into its next instruction when that instruction's domain admits zero, and applies
the instruction to the empty stream. That is how an initial instruction gives its seed, and for a linear instruction
it gives nothing. For an affine instruction it gives something, once for every dead monad, but the frontier does not
record how many monads died: in the monad space, every one of them is $0$. The results below that rest on linearity
therefore concern code whose per-object instructions are linear.

In metatron, `BasicSwarmComputer.resolve` makes one dead monad at each initial instruction. So `s.p`, which runs as
`start(s).p`, begins with a dead monad that `start` turns into $s$. A dead monad that walks on is a zombie
(`Monad.zombie`). `Processor.Helper.invoke` answers `noobj` for an empty input unless the instruction's domain admits
zero, and `map` and `branch` declare the domain $\{?\}$, which does.

::: **Proposition 2** (Bulking collects like terms). Two monads bulk exactly when they are one basis element
$x \otimes j$, and bulking adds their points, $c\,(x \otimes j) + d\,(x \otimes j) = (c + d)\,(x \otimes j)$.
Bulking never changes a frontier as an element of the monad space.
:::

::: *Proof.* The basis of the monad space is the pairs $(x, j)$, and a sum of equal basis elements is their
coefficient sum (Definition 2). ◻
:::

Bulking is a choice of representation. The machine may hold $c\,(x \otimes j)$ as one monad or as $c$ monads, and both
are the same element. Holding it as one is what keeps a frontier small [[5]](#references). Proposition 2 is the bulk
axiom of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md), $\langle cx, dx \rangle = \langle (c+d)x \rangle$, and the first law of points
in [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md), $ca + da = (c + d)a$, read in the monad space.

**Live proof.** Four monads become two, since 1 and 3 have one remainder modulo 2, and 2 and 4 another.

```mtron
mtron> {1,2,3,4}.mod(2)
==>{2}1
==>{2}0
```
In metatron, a monad is the list `[obj, inst, state, code]` (`StatefulMonad`). `Monad.halted()` holds when its
instruction is the `noobj` end, and `Monad.dead()` when its object is `noobj`. Two monads are equal terms when their
components, coefficients included, are equal (`Monad.Helpers.monadEquals`); the state is the subject of *Species of
Monads*. The basis element is the object with its span and without its point: `MObjs.attemptBulk` keys a stream's
members on the object with its point set to one, a spanned object keeping its span, and adds their points, which is
Proposition 2. The machine collects like terms when it builds a stream with `objs(...)`, and the halted result is
built that way. Its running list (`ListMonad`) and a barrier's slice, which grows by `append` and bulks only past
`MObjs.BULK_TRIGGER` members, may hold
like terms uncollected, and that is the same frontier.

# The Step

::: **Definition 10** (Step). Let every instruction of a code $p = i_1 \cdots i_k$ other than its gathers be
per-object. The *step* of $p$ sends a running monad $x \otimes j$ to
$$
\begin{equation*}
T(x \otimes j) = f_{i_j}(x) \otimes \nu(j),
\end{equation*}
$$
fixes every halted and every waiting monad, and extends to a frontier linearly.
:::

The instruction moves the data counter, $x \mapsto f_{i_j}(x)$, and the code moves the program counter,
$j \mapsto \nu(j)$. The step is the sum of the two moves tensored together,
$$
\begin{equation*}
T \;=\; \sum_{j \,\text{running}} \rho(i_j) \otimes E_{\nu(j)\,j}
\;+\; \mathrm{id}_M \otimes \Big(E_{\dashv\,\dashv} + \sum_{b \,\text{gather}} E_{b\,b}\Big),
\end{equation*}
$$
where $E_{j'j}$ sends position $j$ to $j'$ and every other position to zero.

::: **Proposition 3** (The step is linear). For a code as in Definition 10, $T$ is $R$-linear on the monad space. In
particular $T\big(c\,(x \otimes j)\big) = c\,T(x \otimes j)$: the point of a bulked monad rides through its step.
:::

::: *Proof.* $T$ is a sum of tensor products of linear maps. ◻
:::

**Live proof.** Two 3s give two 4s, and the point rides along. A point on the instruction multiplies in, as points
multiply along a chain [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md): three lanes of $+1$ on two 3s give six 4s.

```mtron
mtron> {2}3.plus(1)
==>{2}4
mtron> {2}3.plus{3}(1)
==>{6}4
```
In metatron, `StatefulMonad.next(obj)` returns `[obj, code.nextInst(inst), state, code]`: the data counter set to the
instruction's output, and the program counter set to the code's next instruction. `BasicSwarmComputer.split` hands a
bulked monad to its instruction unsplit when its object is one repeated value and the instruction takes one object at
a time. `Processor.Helper.invoke` then applies the instruction to one copy and multiplies the count back, so the step is
taken once, as Proposition 3 permits. A monad that holds a stream of distinct objects is split first.

# Evaluation

A step moves one instruction from the code onto the data. To say so exactly, read the code as an element of a ring.

::: **Definition 11** (Code ring). Let $I_1$ be the per-object instructions. The *code ring* $F = R\langle I_1 \rangle$
is the free algebra on them: sums of instruction words with coefficients, multiplied by concatenation. Its unity $1$ is
the empty word.
:::

Under the action below, $F$ maps into the ring of stream functions of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md), where the
relations among instructions live. $F$ itself has none: it is the syntax of programs.

$F$ is generated by the per-object instructions because that is where the code is a ring. With its reduce functions the
code is only a near-ring, by Lemma 1 and Theorem 18 of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md): $(a + b)\,c \neq ac + bc$ when
$c$ reduces, as the left side reduces one merged stream and the right side two. The flatmap functions, maps and filters
among them, are closed under both operations, and they are the per-object instructions of Definition 8.

Both coefficients of an instruction are already in $F$ [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md). A point is a
scalar, `a{c}` being $c\,a$, and a span is a power, `{n}a` being $a^n$ and `{m,n}a` the sum $a^m + \cdots + a^n$. So
`{n}a{c}` is $c\,a^n$: the point iterates the $+$ of $F$, and the span its $\cdot$. A spanned instruction holds one
position, and the machine walks its run when it applies it [[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md), so one step
moves the whole word $a^n$ across the tensor sign.

**Live proof.** Five steps of $+1$ from 0 are one instruction, and three lanes of the run $+34$, $+34$ from 1 land on
69 three times.

```mtron
mtron> 0.{5}plus(1)
==>5
mtron> 1.{2}plus{3}(34)
==>{3}69
```
::: **Proposition 4** (Data is a module over code). Setting $x \cdot i = \rho(i)(x)$ for each per-object instruction
$i$, and extending by
$$
\begin{equation*}
x \cdot (i_1\,i_2 \cdots i_k) = \Big(\big((x \cdot i_1) \cdot i_2\big) \cdots\Big) \cdot i_k,
\qquad
x \cdot (w + w') = x \cdot w + x \cdot w',
\end{equation*}
$$
makes $M$ a right $F$-module.
:::

::: *Proof.* $F$ is free on $I_1$, so an assignment of a linear map to each instruction extends uniquely to words, by
composition in their order, and to sums, by addition [[8]](#references). ◻
:::

A program applied to data is the module action, so the mtron expression `s.p` is $s \cdot p$. The action is on the
right because mtron reads a code from left to right, and its first instruction acts first. This answers, in part, the
module of data over code that [[4]](./ring-theoretic-distributed-structures.md) asks for in its mirror of distributed
computing: here a program acts on data through its per-object instructions, and the gathers of *Barriers* act outside
the module.

A *branch* is an instruction whose members are code. When its members are code of per-object instructions, it is
per-object, and its action is the sum of its members' actions,
$\rho\big(\mathrm{branch}(p_1, \ldots, p_n)\big) = \rho(p_1) + \cdots + \rho(p_n)$, the $+$ of $F$ acting on data. Its
domain is $\{?\}$, so it is applied to the empty stream as well, and a branch of linear members is linear. A branch
feeds its members one object at a time, so a member that gathers folds each object alone: there is no nested reduce
function (Proposition 1 of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md)).

**Live proof.** The two members of a branch take 1 to 2 by different routes, and their monads meet as one basis
element.

```mtron
mtron> 1.branch(plus(1),mult(2))
==>{2}2
```
::: **Definition 12** (Remainder and evaluation). For a code of per-object instructions, the *remainder* of position
$j$ is the word the program counter has left to run, $w_j = i_j\,i_{j+1} \cdots i_k \in F$, with $w_\dashv = 1$. The
*evaluation* of a monad is $\mathrm{ev}(x \otimes j) = x \cdot w_j$, extended linearly.
:::

::: **Theorem 1** (The step preserves evaluation). For a code of linear per-object instructions,
$\mathrm{ev} \circ T = \mathrm{ev}$, and $\mathrm{ev}(x \otimes \dashv) = x$.
:::

::: *Proof.* For a running monad,
$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{lclll}
\mathrm{ev}\big(T(x \otimes j)\big) &=& \mathrm{ev}\big(f_{i_j}(x) \otimes \nu(j)\big) && [\text{Definition 10}] \\
&=& (x \cdot i_j) \cdot w_{\nu(j)} && [\text{Definition 12}] \\
&=& x \cdot (i_j\,w_{\nu(j)}) && [\text{Proposition 4}] \\
&=& x \cdot w_j && [w_j = i_j\,w_{\nu(j)}].
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
$$
For a halted monad, $T$ is the identity and $w_\dashv = 1$. Both sides are linear, so the equation holds on every
frontier. ◻
:::

::: **Theorem 2** (The machine computes $M \otimes_F F \cong M$). Let $p$ be a code of linear per-object
instructions, and send a monad $x \otimes j$ to $x \otimes w_j$ in $M \otimes_F F$. A step is then the balancing
relation of Definition 4, applied to the first letter of the remainder,
$$
\begin{equation*}
x \otimes \big(i_j\,w_{\nu(j)}\big) = \big(x \cdot i_j\big) \otimes w_{\nu(j)},
\end{equation*}
$$
so every frontier of a computation of $s$ under $p$ is sent to the same element, $s \otimes p$. A halted frontier is
sent to $(s \cdot p) \otimes 1$, and its data is the image of $s \otimes p$ under $M \otimes_F F \cong M$: the result
is $s \cdot p$.
:::

::: *Proof.* The displayed equation is Definition 4 with $f = i_j$, read through Definitions 10 and 12, since
$w_j = i_j\,w_{\nu(j)}$. By Theorem 1, the evaluation of the frontier is the same at every step. At a halted frontier
every remainder is $1$, and $m \otimes 1$ is the image of $m$. ◻
:::

The machine computes nothing the algebra does not already say. It moves instructions across the tensor sign, one per
step, until every remainder is the unity, and then the data is the answer.

# The Result

::: **Definition 13** (Result). The *result* of a halted frontier $\sum c\,(x \otimes \dashv)$ is its data,
$\sum c\,x \in M$, the image under the isomorphism $M \otimes_R R\dashv \cong M$ that sends $x \otimes \dashv$ to $x$.
:::

A halted monad is its object. Once the program counter reaches the end, the program factor has one dimension, and the
tensor with it is the object itself. A halted frontier holds a single position, so only its data counters can differ,
and two monads that reach the same object bulk however they got there. A barrier's slice has the same shape (Definition
17).

**Live proof.** A chain of two maps halts at three objects.

```mtron
mtron> {1,2,3}.plus(1).mult(2)
==>4
==>6
==>8
```
In metatron, a halted monad's objects are handed to the computer's halt hook (`BasicSwarmComputer.makeOnHalt`), which
appends them to the halted pool. The result is `objs(halted)`, which bulks as it is built.

# Species of Monads

A monad may carry more than its two counters: a record of its walk, or a loop counter. Each adds a factor to the monad
space, and with it a coordinate that two monads must share to bulk.

::: **Definition 14** (State). A *state space* is a set $S$, and a *stateful* monad is a basis element
$x \otimes j \otimes \sigma$ of $M \otimes_R N \otimes_R R[S]$. The step acts on the state by a rule of its own,
$T(x \otimes j \otimes \sigma) = \sum_y c_y\,\big(y \otimes \nu(j) \otimes \sigma_y\big)$ when
$f_{i_j}(x) = \sum_y c_y\,y$.
:::

::: **Definition 15** (Walks). A *walk* is the word of objects that a monad's data counter has held,
$x_0\,x_1 \cdots x_t$. The *walk algebra* is the free algebra $R\langle X \rangle$. A *path-tracking* monad carries its
walk as its state, and each step extends the walk by the new data counter: $\sigma_y = \sigma \cdot y$.
:::

The walk algebra is a free algebra again. It records everything, and two walks are equal only when they are the same
word. A stateless monad is its quotient. A walk is a whole object thread of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md), its
*mutation history* $\Delta$ followed by its datum: a map takes $(x, \Delta)$ to $(a(x), \Delta \uplus x)$, and a reducer
resets the history. There a filter leaves the history as it is, where Definition 15 extends the walk at every step.

::: **Proposition 5** (A stateless monad is a walk of length one). Dropping the state, $x \otimes j \otimes \sigma
\mapsto x \otimes j$, is a linear surjection of the stateful monad space onto the stateless one, and it commutes with
the step. The last object of a monad's walk is its data counter, so a stateless monad remembers its walk only as far
as its current object.
:::

::: *Proof.* The map is $\mathrm{id} \otimes \mathrm{id} \otimes \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon : R[S] \to R$ sends
every state to $1$, so it is linear and onto. Both ways round, $x \otimes j \otimes \sigma$ is sent to
$\sum_y c_y\,\big(y \otimes \nu(j)\big)$. ◻
:::

The species form a chain of quotients, and bulking grows down the chain. Two path-tracking monads bulk only when their
walks agree, while two stateless monads bulk whenever their current object and position agree.

**Live proof.** From 1 and 3, the walks through $+1$ and then $\bmod\, 2$ end at the same 0, so the two stateless
monads bulk. Their walks, $1\,2\,0$ and $3\,4\,0$, differ, so two path-tracking monads would stay apart.

```mtron
mtron> {1,3}.plus(1).mod(2)
==>{2}0
```
[To be shown: the path-tracking run, once `path()` carries the walk of Definition 15.]

In metatron, a monad's state is a record, `StatefulMonad.state()`, and a loop counter is `state/loop`. Path tracking,
`state/path`, does not yet carry the walk of Definition 15.

# Barriers

A per-object instruction never needs to see more than one monad. A gather needs every monad that reaches it, and the
machine makes that possible by treating a stream as an object.

::: **Definition 16** (Streams are objects). A stream is itself an object: there is a map $\alpha : M \to X$, with
$\alpha(x) = x$ for a nonempty object $x$ and $\alpha(0) = \mathtt{noobj}$, so a single object is the stream that holds
it once and the empty stream is `noobj`. A stream of streams is its members:
$$
\begin{equation*}
\alpha\Big(\sum_s c_s\,\alpha(s)\Big) = \alpha\Big(\sum_s c_s\,s\Big).
\end{equation*}
$$
:::

These two laws make $\alpha$ an algebra, in the sense of Eilenberg and Moore, for the monad that sends a pointed set to
the free module on its points other than the base point [[9,10]](#references). The algebras of that monad are the
$R$-modules, abelian groups when $R$ is the integers. metatron states the second law as "there is no nested stream".
Because a stream is an object, every instruction is a total function on streams. A per-object instruction is fixed by
its values on single objects, and a gather may be any total function.

::: **Definition 17** (Slice). For a gather at position $b$, the *slice* of a frontier at $b$ is its component there,
$m_b \otimes b$ with $m_b \in M$.
:::

A slice is a pure tensor. Every monad that reaches $b$ shares the program counter $b$, so a slice is one stream
tensored with one position, and only data counters can differ.

::: **Theorem 3** (Why a barrier waits). A gather's value on a slice may depend on the whole slice, so the machine
applies it when the slice is complete: when no running monad can still reach it. Let the gather be a fold, whose
answer is $\varphi_b(m_b)$ for a map $\varphi_b$ into a commutative monoid $\langle A, \oplus \rangle$. If
$\varphi_b(m + m') = \varphi_b(m) \oplus \varphi_b(m')$, the slice may be folded as its monads arrive, and the barrier
waits only to emit.
:::

::: *Proof.* A monad that reaches $b$ after the gather is applied adds a term to $m_b$, and $G_b(m_b + m)$ need not be
a function of $G_b(m_b)$ and $m$: the streams $\{1, 2\}$ and $\{0, 3\}$ have the same median, $1.5$, but with a $3$
added their medians are $2$ and $3$. For an additive fold, $\varphi_b(m_b + m) = \varphi_b(m_b) \oplus \varphi_b(m)$,
so the barrier may hold the fold of the monads that have arrived and add each arrival to it, and only the answer waits
for the slice to be complete. A map on single objects into a commutative monoid extends uniquely to such a
homomorphism on slices with natural coefficients, by the universal property of the free commutative monoid
$\mathbb{N}[X]$. A slice with negative coefficients needs $\langle A, \oplus \rangle$ to be an abelian group, by the
universal property of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ [[9]](#references). ◻
:::

The first half of Theorem 3 is Theorem 16 of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md), *reduce functions are temporal*: every
function before a reducer is applied, and its streams merged, before the reducer reads its input. The second half is
the license that `law_shard` reads in [[3]](./ring-theoretic-distributed-computing.md): a fold that declares a
commutative monoid can be pushed into the branches of its input. There it follows from Theorem 19 of
[[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md), and here from the universal property that makes the fold a homomorphism.

::: **Definition 18** (Drain). When no monad is running, the machine drains the barriers in code order. *Draining* the
barrier at $b$ replaces its slice with the gather's output at the next position, and the monads it makes start with an
empty state: $m_b \otimes b \mapsto G_b(m_b) \otimes \nu(b)$. A barrier is drained even when its slice is empty, so a
seeded fold of nothing gives its seed, $G_b(0)$, while a gather whose domain is $\{+\}$ gives nothing. The instruction
at $\nu(b)$ decides the shape of the output: a gather takes it into its own slice, a batching instruction takes it as
one monad, and any other instruction takes one monad per object, a *scatter*.
:::

::: **Theorem 4** (Strata). Let a code without loops hold gathers at positions $b_1 < \cdots < b_m$, with every other
instruction per-object and linear, and let $L$ run the step until no monad is running. A computation of $s$ is the
composite
$$
\begin{equation*}
s \;\mapsto\; L\Big(D_{b_m}\big(\cdots L\big(D_{b_1}\big(L(s \otimes 1)\big)\big) \cdots\big)\Big),
\end{equation*}
$$
where $D_b$ drains the barrier at $b$. Each $L$ is a power of $T$, so it is linear (Proposition 3), and only the
drains may be non-linear.
:::

::: *Proof.* $T$ fixes halted and waiting monads, and every step moves a running monad's program counter forward, so in
a code without loops no monad is running after at most $k$ steps; $L$ is that power of $T$. Every monad is then halted
or waiting at a barrier not yet drained, and program counters only move forward, so nothing can still reach the first
of those barriers: its slice is complete, as Theorem 3 requires. The machine drains it and runs the step again. ◻
:::

A computation alternates linear phases with barrier drains. The linear phases need no coordination, since a linear
map splits over any division of its input, and the drains are where the computation synchronizes: the cuts of
[[3]](./ring-theoretic-distributed-computing.md). Stratified Datalog evaluates negation stratum by stratum in the same
way [[11]](#references), and the supersteps of bulk synchronous parallel computation are strata
[[12]](#references).

**Live proof.** A sum drains once, after its stream is complete. A count of three objects is 3, and a single object is
the stream that holds it once, so its count is 1. A sort drains its stream to one list, and the merge after it
scatters the list back into monads.

```mtron
mtron> {1,2,3}.plus(1).sum()
==>9
mtron> {1,1,2}.count()
==>3
mtron> 5.count()
==>1
mtron> {3,1,2}.order().merge()
==>1
==>2
==>3
```
In metatron, `BasicSwarmComputer.resolve` creates one barrier monad per gather, in code order, each holding an empty
stream. An arriving monad appends its object to the first barrier's stream, and a dead one appends `noobj`, which
changes nothing. When the running list is empty, the first barrier is taken and its instruction applied to its stream,
empty or not. The output goes to the next barrier, into one monad, or scattered, by `Inst.isGather` and
`Inst.isBatching`.

# The Machine

The four parts assemble into the loop `BasicSwarmComputer` runs.

1. **Start.** Make an empty slice at every gather, and a dead monad at every initial instruction, which that
   instruction turns into its seed. A code with no initial instruction is seeded with the start, $s \otimes 1$.
2. **Step.** While some monad is running, step one (Definition 10) and route each of its successors by its position:
   into its barrier's slice at a gather, to the halted frontier at $\dashv$, and back to the running frontier
   otherwise. A dead successor vanishes, unless its next instruction's domain admits zero, and then it walks on.
3. **Drain.** When no monad is running and a barrier remains, drain the first one (Definition 18), even if its slice
   is empty, and return to the step.
4. **Result.** When neither remains, the result is the data of the halted frontier (Definition 13).

A code without loops halts: every step moves a running monad's program counter forward. A loop revisits positions,
and then the run is a sum of powers of the step, as a span range is a sum of powers of its instruction. It halts when
the loop's frontier empties, as the walk of a span halts where its run leaves the domain
[[2]](./span-and-point-coefficient-algebra.md).

In metatron, the loop is `BasicSwarmComputer.runMonads`. It also counts failed monads and stops after ten, an
engineering bound that lies outside this algebra.

# Conclusion

**NOTES**:

- A monad is a basis element of $M \otimes_R N$: an object tensored with an instruction position. Bulking adds the
  points of like terms, a dead monad is zero by bilinearity, and a halted monad is its object.
- The step tensors the instruction's move of the data counter with the code's move of the program counter. For linear
  instructions it is linear, and the point of a bulked monad rides through it.
- The data module is a right module over the code ring of per-object instructions. Between barriers, a computation
  moves instructions across the tensor sign, one per step, and computes the canonical isomorphism
  $M \otimes_F F \cong M$; its result is $s \cdot p$.
- metatron's two coefficients sit in the two rings. The point is the scalar of data and of code, which bulking adds and
  a step multiplies. The span is a power in the code ring, a run the machine walks in one step, and on a value it is
  part of the object.
- An instruction that gives something from nothing is affine. The machine applies it by walking dead monads, and the
  module does not record how many died; that is where the machine and its algebra part.
- State adds tensor factors. Walks form a free algebra, and a stateless monad is its quotient, a walk of length one.
  Bulking grows as the state is forgotten.
- A stream is an object, by the algebra laws of the free-module monad, so every instruction is a total function on
  streams. A per-object instruction is fixed by its values on single objects, and a gather need not be linear.
- A barrier waits because a gather may need its whole slice. A fold into a commutative monoid is a homomorphism, on
  slices with natural coefficients or into an abelian group, so it may fold on arrival and across machines. A
  computation is a sequence of strata: linear phases separated by drains.
- The point coefficients are a parameter of the algebra. The machine's are the integers, and with them it counts; over
  other rings the same algebra weighs or interferes, and with complex points and unitary steps it is the quantum walk
  of [[6]](#references). [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md) carries that walk's coin in a $\mathbb{C}^{2 \times 2}$
  coefficient, where bulking is interference.
- Open, from the *sacks* of [[1]](./stream-ring-theory.md): objects that bulk merge their sacks with an operator, each
  keeping its coefficient apart from its sack. That is no tensor factor: in $M \otimes N \otimes A$ a point passes into
  the state, $c\,(x \otimes j \otimes \sigma) = x \otimes j \otimes (c\sigma)$, which is right for a coin's amplitudes
  and wrong for a sack. A sack merge keeps the count and the state apart: a species still to be stated.
