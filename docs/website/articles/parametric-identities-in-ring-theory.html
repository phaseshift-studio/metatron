    • Parametric Identities in Ring Theory

    An identity can be a parametric instruction, one finite term that stands for an infinite unity, such as the diagonal relation, at every type.
    Dr. Marko A. Rodriguez
    Dr. Stynx

    Introduction

    The identities of a classical ring are values. The zero \(0\) and the unity \(1\) are elements of the ring's set, and \(0 + a = a\) and \(1 \cdot a = a\) hold by their place in the algebra. Stream Ring Theory [1] made the identities functions. Its function ring \(\langle\mathcal{F}, +, \cdot \rangle\) branches with \(+\) and composes with \(\cdot\). Its zero \(0 (x) = \emptyset\) outputs no object, and its unity \(1 (x) = x\) outputs the object it is given. In mtron, the language of metatron, the zero is noobj and the unity is id() [2].

    mtron is a code-as-data language: an instruction can stand wherever a value stands, at either end of an arrow included. A ring of data therefore need not hold its identities as values. It can take them from the function ring, and this matters when the identity the data ring needs is an infinite value. Relations under composition are such a ring. Their unity is the diagonal, the relation of every object to itself, and over infinitely many objects no value holds it. The arrow \(1 \Rightarrow 1\), from the unity of the function ring to itself, is one finite term that stands for the diagonal. The unity is parametric, \(1 : \forall A.\, A \to A\), so the one arrow is the unity of relations between objects of every type, and parametricity, not a check at each type, guarantees that it is.

    This article defines the relation ring and proves that no value is its unity (Theorem 1), that the arrow \(1 \Rightarrow 1\) is its unity (Theorem 2), and that parametricity makes it the unity at every type (Theorem 3). It then finds a second parametric identity, the unit of the product of lsts, which is parametric in the object it multiplies rather than in its type (Theorems 4 and 5).

    Live proof. An arrow composes with an arrow that starts where it ends, \((2 \Rightarrow 3) \cdot (3 \Rightarrow 4) = 2 \Rightarrow 4\). The unity of a relation is the arrow between two identity instructions, and composing with it leaves the relation as it was: \((1 \Rightarrow 1) \cdot (2 \Rightarrow 3) = 2 \Rightarrow 3\).

    mtron> (2=>3) * (3=>4)
==>2=>4
mtron> (2=>3).one()
==>id?A<=A()=>id?A<=A()
mtron> (2=>3).one() * (2=>3)
==>2=>3

    The Relation Ring

    The objects, coefficients and functions are those of [1]: \(x, y, z \in X\) are objects, \(c, d \in \mathcal{C}\) are coefficients of a ring with unity, and \(a, b \in \mathcal{F}\) are stream functions.

    ::: Definition 1 (Relation). If \(x, y \in X\), then the arrow \(x \Rightarrow y\) relates \(x\) to \(y\). Arrows multiply by composition: \[ \begin{equation*} (x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (y' \Rightarrow z) = \begin{cases} x \Rightarrow z & \text{if $y = y'$}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \end{equation*} \] A relation \(r \in \mathcal{R}\) is a finite sum of arrows with coefficients, \(r = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i (x_i \Rightarrow y_i)\), and the empty sum is \(0\). Relations multiply arrow by arrow, the coefficients multiplying as in the stream ring [1], \(c (x \Rightarrow y) \cdot d (y' \Rightarrow z) = (c \cdot d) ((x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (y' \Rightarrow z))\), so that \(\cdot\) distributes over \(+\). :::

    With coefficients in \(\mathcal{C}\), \(\langle\mathcal{R}, +, \cdot \rangle\) is the ring of finitely supported matrices over \(\mathcal{C}\) whose rows and columns are indexed by \(X\). The arrow \(x \Rightarrow y\) is the matrix with a \(1\) at row \(x\) and column \(y\) and \(0\) elsewhere.

    Live proof. Arrows that meet compose, \((2 \Rightarrow 3) \cdot (3 \Rightarrow 4) \cdot (4 \Rightarrow 5) = 2 \Rightarrow 5\), and arrows that do not meet compose to the zero, \((2 \Rightarrow 3) \cdot (4 \Rightarrow 5) = 0\), which outputs nothing and counts nothing. A sum multiplies arrow by arrow: \(((2 \Rightarrow 3) + (4 \Rightarrow 5)) \cdot (3 \Rightarrow 6) = (2 \Rightarrow 6) + 0 = 2 \Rightarrow 6\).

    mtron> (2=>3) * (3=>4) * (4=>5)
==>2=>5
mtron> (2=>3) * (4=>5)
mtron> (2=>3).mult((4=>5)).count()
==>0
mtron> (2=>3).plus((4=>5)) * (3=>6)
==>2=>6

    In mtron, an arrow is a rel, x=>y, and a sum of rels is a stream of them. mult composes and plus sums, written infix as * and + with a space on each side, as a * with no space is a dereference. A rel argument takes parentheses of its own, mult((4=>5)).

    ::: Theorem 1 (No value is the unity). If \(X\) is infinite, then no relation \(e \in \mathcal{R}\) satisfies \(r \cdot e = r\) for every \(r \in \mathcal{R}\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(e = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i (x_i \Rightarrow y_i)\), then \(e\) names finitely many objects. As \(X\) is infinite, there is an object \(u \in X\) with \(u \neq x_i\) for every \(i\). Then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (u \Rightarrow u) \cdot e &=& \sum_{i=1}^{n} (u \Rightarrow u) \cdot c_i (x_i \Rightarrow y_i) && [\text{$\cdot$ is left distributive}] \\ &=& \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i ((u \Rightarrow u) \cdot (x_i \Rightarrow y_i)) && [\text{Definition 1}] \\ &=& \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\, 0 && [\text{$u \neq x_i$, Definition 1}] \\ &=& 0, && \end{array} \end{equation*} \] and \(0 \neq u \Rightarrow u\). ◻ :::

    The unity that composition needs is the diagonal, the sum of the arrows from every object to itself: \[ \begin{equation*} \Delta_X = \sum_{x \in X} (x \Rightarrow x). \end{equation*} \] It is the identity matrix. In \((x \Rightarrow y) \cdot \Delta_X\), the one term that survives is \(y \Rightarrow y\), and in \(\Delta_X \cdot (x \Rightarrow y)\), it is \(x \Rightarrow x\), so both products are \(x \Rightarrow y\). Over an infinite \(X\), \(\Delta_X\) is an infinite sum, which is why Theorem 1 finds no relation that is a unity: \(\mathcal{R}\) needs a unity that no value can hold.

    Parametric Identities

    ::: Definition 2 (Parametric identities). The parametric unity of \(\mathcal{R}\) is the arrow \(1 \Rightarrow 1\) with the unity \(1 \in \mathcal{F}\) at both ends, and the parametric zero is the arrow \(0 \Rightarrow 0\) with the zero \(0 \in \mathcal{F}\) at both ends. Each stands for the arrows between the outputs of its function on every object \(x \in X\). As \(1 (x) = x\), \[ \begin{equation*} 1 \Rightarrow 1 = \sum_{x \in X} (1 (x) \Rightarrow 1 (x)) = \sum_{x \in X} (x \Rightarrow x) = \Delta_X. \end{equation*} \] As \(0 (x) = \emptyset\) outputs no object, \(0 \Rightarrow 0\) stands for no arrow and is the empty sum \(0\). :::

    The zero is a finite value either way. It is the unity that needs a finite name, and the parametric unity is the diagonal written as one arrow. \(\Delta_X\) is the graph of the function \(1\), and the function is finite where its graph is not. \(\Delta_X\) is not a relation of \(\mathcal{R}\), but it multiplies as one, arrow by arrow, and against any one arrow all but one of its terms are \(0\).

    ::: Theorem 2 (The parametric unity is the unity of \(\mathcal{R}\)). If \(r \in \mathcal{R}\), then \(r \cdot (1 \Rightarrow 1) = (1 \Rightarrow 1) \cdot r = r\). :::

    ::: Proof. As \(\cdot\) distributes over \(+\), it suffices to prove the equalities for an arrow \(x \Rightarrow y\). Then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (1 \Rightarrow 1) &=& \sum_{z \in X} (x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (1 (z) \Rightarrow 1 (z)) && [\text{Definition 2, $\cdot$ distributes}] \\ &=& (x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (1 (y) \Rightarrow 1 (y)) && [\text{$1 (z) = y$ only at $z = y$, Definition 1}] \\ &=& (x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (y \Rightarrow y) && [1 (y) = y] \\ &=& x \Rightarrow y && [\text{Definition 1}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] On the left, the sum collapses at \(x\), where the arrow starts: \((1 \Rightarrow 1) \cdot (x \Rightarrow y) = (1 (x) \Rightarrow 1 (x)) \cdot (x \Rightarrow y) = x \Rightarrow y\). ◻ :::

    In the second line, the sum over all of \(X\) collapses to its one term at \(y\), the object where the arrow ends. The product evaluates the unity at \(y\) and nowhere else, so the diagonal is never enumerated. \(\mathcal{R}\) has no unity among its values (Theorem 1), and the function ring lends it one.

    In mtron, one() asked of a rel is id()=>id(), printed id?A<=A()=>id?A<=A(): the identity instruction, whose domain and range are the generic A, at both ends. mult recognizes the identity arrow by its instruction and returns the other operand, which is the collapse in the proof of Theorem 2. zero() is noobj=>noobj, which mtron reads as noobj: it prints nothing, drops out of a sum, and counts as nothing.

    Live proof. The unity on the right, \((2 \Rightarrow 3) \cdot (1 \Rightarrow 1) = 2 \Rightarrow 3\), and in the middle of a chain, where it composes away: \((2 \Rightarrow 3) \cdot (1 \Rightarrow 1) \cdot (3 \Rightarrow 4) = 2 \Rightarrow 4\). The zero counts nothing, adds nothing, \((2 \Rightarrow 3) + 0 = 2 \Rightarrow 3\), and annihilates, \((2 \Rightarrow 3) \cdot 0 = 0\).

    mtron> (2=>3).mult((2=>3).one())
==>2=>3
mtron> (2=>3) * (id()=>id()) * (3=>4)
==>2=>4
mtron> (2=>3).zero().count()
==>0
mtron> (2=>3) + (noobj=>noobj)
==>2=>3
mtron> (2=>3).mult((noobj=>noobj)).count()
==>0

    Parametricity

    The diagonal is large in two ways: a type may have infinitely many objects, and there are infinitely many types. The parametric unity compresses it twice. The first compression is the function. The diagonal of a type \(A\) is the graph of the identity on \(A\), \(\lambda x{:}A.\, x\), one finite rule for every arrow of the diagonal. The second compression is polymorphism. An ad hoc unity is a family of functions, one per type, \(\{1_A\}_A\), as many as there are types [3]. A parametric unity is one term of System F [4, 5]: \[ \begin{equation*} 1 = \Lambda A.\, \lambda x{:}A.\, x \quad : \quad \forall A.\, A \to A. \end{equation*} \] The quantifier \(\forall A\) ranges over every type without enumerating them. As a map, one object in and one object out, the unity of [1] has this type, so the one arrow \(1 \Rightarrow 1\) is the unity of relations between objects of every type.

    Live proof. One instruction is the unity of the function ring on objects of every type, \(\langle x \rangle 1 = \langle x \rangle\) for an int, a str and a lst \(x\). One arrow is the unity of relations between objects of every type, \((x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (1 \Rightarrow 1) = x \Rightarrow y\) for a str \(x\) and a bool \(y\), and for a lst \(x\) and a real \(y\).

    mtron> 5.id()
==>5
mtron> 'a'.id()
==>'a'
mtron> [2,'a'].id()
==>[2,'a']
mtron> ('a'=>true) * (id()=>id())
==>'a'=>true
mtron> ([2,3]=>4.5) * (id()=>id())
==>[2,3]=>4.5

    One term serves every type because a parametric function cannot inspect the type it is instantiated at. A total function of type \(\forall A.\, A \to A\) can do nothing with its object but return it [6]. Wadler's free theorems make the argument short [7].

    ::: Theorem 3 (The type entails the unity). If \(f : \forall A.\, A \to A\) is total and parametric, then \(f_A (x) = x\) for every type \(A\) and every object \(x\) of type \(A\). :::

    ::: Proof. The free theorem of the type \(\forall A.\, A \to A\) is \(f_C \circ g = g \circ f_B\) for all types \(B\) and \(C\) and every function \(g : B \to C\) [7]. Let \(\{\star\}\) be a type with one object. As \(f_{\{\star\}}\) is total and \(\star\) is the only object of its type, \(f_{\{\star\}} (\star) = \star\). If \(g : \{\star\} \to A\) is \(g (\star) = x\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} f_A (x) &=& f_A (g (\star)) && [g (\star) = x] \\ &=& g (f_{\{\star\}} (\star)) && [\text{free theorem}] \\ &=& g (\star) && [f_{\{\star\}} (\star) = \star] \\ &=& x && [g (\star) = x]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    So the unit laws of \(1 \Rightarrow 1\) are not checked type by type: the type \(\forall A.\, A \to A\) admits no other total parametric term. This is a stronger uniqueness than the classical one. The classical argument, \(e = e \cdot e' = e'\), shows that a ring has one unity. Theorem 3 shows that a parametric unity has one term. The free theorem is the naturality of \(f\) as a transformation of the identity functor on types, so the identities \(\{1_A\}_A\), one per type, form one natural transformation, and Theorem 3 says it is the only one.

    In mtron, an instruction can test the type of its object at run time, so a domain and range of A do not make an instruction parametric. id() is parametric because its body never inspects its object. Theorem 3 holds of the term. Only in a calculus without type inspection, such as System F, does it hold of the type alone.

    Neither parametric identity depends on the relation it is asked of. one() answers the same arrow for a relation between strs as for a relation between ints, and so does zero(). metatron's AbstractAlgebraTest pins this independence from the element as the parametric law of every carrier with a sum and a product.

    Live proof. The unity asked of a relation between strs is the unity of a relation between ints, \((1 \Rightarrow 1) \cdot (2 \Rightarrow 3) = 2 \Rightarrow 3\), and the zero asked of it counts nothing.

    mtron> ('a'=>'b').one()
==>id?A<=A()=>id?A<=A()
mtron> ('a'=>'b').one() * (2=>3)
==>2=>3
mtron> ('a'=>'b').zero().count()
==>0

    A Unit Parametric in its Operand

    A lst is a word of slots, \([x_1, \ldots, x_m]\). Lsts add by concatenation, \([x_1, \ldots, x_m] + [y_1, \ldots, y_n] = [x_1, \ldots, x_m, y_1, \ldots, y_n]\), and the empty lst \([\,]\) is their zero. A product of lsts that distributes over concatenation on the right is fixed by what it does to lsts of one slot, and the product that keeps each slot's own algebra multiplies the slots.

    ::: Definition 3 (Slot product). If \(u = [x_1, \ldots, x_m]\) and \(v = [y_1, \ldots, y_n]\), then \[ \begin{equation*} u \cdot v = [x_1 \cdot y_1, \ldots, x_1 \cdot y_n, \; \ldots, \; x_m \cdot y_1, \ldots, x_m \cdot y_n], \end{equation*} \] where each \(x_i \cdot y_j\) is the product of its slots' type, and a product that yields no object is no slot. :::

    The slot product distributes over concatenation exactly on the right, \((u + u') \cdot v = u \cdot v + u' \cdot v\), and on the left only up to the order of the slots. It is associative wherever the products of the slots are, as the slots of \((u \cdot v) \cdot w\) and of \(u \cdot (v \cdot w)\) come in the same order. Its unit would be a lst of one slot, \([e]\), with \(x \cdot e = e \cdot x = x\) for every slot \(x\), and no value is that slot.

    ::: Theorem 4 (No value is the unit of the slot product). No lst is the unit of the slot product for both the lsts of ints and the lsts of bools. :::

    ::: Proof. Let \(u\) be such a unit. A product of two ints always yields an object, so \([2] \cdot u\) has as many slots as \(u\), and \([2] \cdot u = [2]\) gives \(u\) one slot, \(u = [e]\). For its products with ints, \(e\) is an int, and \([1_{\mathbb{Z}}] \cdot [e] = [1_{\mathbb{Z}}]\) gives \(e = 1_{\mathbb{Z}} \cdot e = 1_{\mathbb{Z}}\). For its products with bools, \(e\) is likewise the bool \(1_{\mathbb{B}} = \top\). No value is both. ◻ :::

    So the unit of the slot product is a family again, \(\{[1_A]\}_A\), one lst per slot type, and an instruction compresses it: the one that answers the one of whatever it meets.

    ::: Definition 4 (The parametric one). The instruction \(\mathrm{one}\) takes the slot it meets to the one of that slot's type: if \(x : A\), then \(\mathrm{one}(x) = 1_A\). In a slot product it meets the facing slot, on either side: \[ \begin{equation*} x \cdot \mathrm{one} = x \cdot \mathrm{one}(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{one} \cdot x = \mathrm{one}(x) \cdot x. \end{equation*} \] :::

    ::: Theorem 5 (The parametric one is the unit of the slot product). If the slot types of a lst \(u\) are monoids under their products, then \(u \cdot [\mathrm{one}] = [\mathrm{one}] \cdot u = u\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(u = [x_1, \ldots, x_m]\) with \(x_i : A_i\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} u \cdot [\mathrm{one}] &=& [x_1 \cdot \mathrm{one}, \ldots, x_m \cdot \mathrm{one}] && [\text{Definition 3}] \\ &=& [x_1 \cdot \mathrm{one}(x_1), \ldots, x_m \cdot \mathrm{one}(x_m)] && [\text{Definition 4}] \\ &=& [x_1 \cdot 1_{A_1}, \ldots, x_m \cdot 1_{A_m}] && [\mathrm{one}(x) = 1_A] \\ &=& [x_1, \ldots, x_m] && [\text{$1_{A_i}$ is the unity of $A_i$}], \end{array} \end{equation*} \] and \([\mathrm{one}] \cdot u = u\) by the same steps on the left. ◻ :::

    The one is parametric in another sense than \(1 \Rightarrow 1\). The unity \(1\) is parametric in the type: one implementation, return the object, serves every type alike, and Theorem 3 makes it the only total term of its type. The instruction \(\mathrm{one}\) is parametric in its operand: the slot it meets selects the one of its own type. Its implementation differs from type to type, \(1_{\mathbb{Z}}\), \(1_{\mathbb{R}}\), \(\top\) and \(1 \Rightarrow 1\), ad hoc in Strachey's sense [3], while the term is one. This is the polymorphism of type classes [8], whose translation types it as \(\mathrm{one} : \forall A.\, \mathrm{Monoid}(A) \to A\), a function of the monoid structure that the operand supplies. Its correctness is therefore not a free theorem but the unit law of each type, which is what metatron's AbstractAlgebraTest checks carrier by carrier. The two kinds nest: in a lst of arrows, \(\mathrm{one}\) meets an arrow and becomes \(1 \Rightarrow 1\), the one parametric in the operand handing over to the one parametric in the type.

    As a product that yields no object is no slot, a lst of arrows multiplies as a relation: \([a \Rightarrow 2, b \Rightarrow 3] \cdot [2 \Rightarrow x, 3 \Rightarrow y] = [a \Rightarrow x, b \Rightarrow y]\), the two arrows that do not meet leaving no slot. Its unit is \([\mathrm{one}]\), whose slot is \(1 \Rightarrow 1\) wherever it meets an arrow.

    In mtron, one() asked of any lst is [one()], a lst whose one slot holds the one() instruction. mult returns the facing slot wherever a slot holds one(), which is what evaluating it against that slot would give, and multiplies every other pair of slots by their own mult. On its own, one() in an argument is evaluated against the lhs, so 2.mult(one()) is 2. A lst of rels is written with each rel in parentheses, [(a=>2),(b=>3)], as [a=>2,b=>3] is a rec.

    Live proof. The one meets an int, a bool and an arrow and becomes the one of each: \(2 \cdot \mathrm{one} = 2\), \(\top \cdot \mathrm{one} = \top\) and \((a \Rightarrow 2) \cdot \mathrm{one} = a \Rightarrow 2\).

    mtron> 2.mult(one())
==>2
mtron> true.mult(one())
==>true
mtron> (a=>2).mult(one())
==>a=>2

    Live proof. The slot product of lsts of ints, its unit on either side, the same unit for a lst of reals, and the one asked of a lst of strs: \([2, 3] \cdot [4, 5] = [8, 10, 12, 15]\), \([2, 3] \cdot [\mathrm{one}] = [\mathrm{one}] \cdot [2, 3] = [2, 3]\) and \([2.5] \cdot [\mathrm{one}] = [2.5]\).

    mtron> [2,3].mult([4,5])
==>[8,10,12,15]
mtron> [2,3].mult([one()])
==>[2,3]
mtron> [one()].mult([2,3])
==>[2,3]
mtron> [2.5].mult([one()])
==>[2.5]
mtron> ['a','b'].one()
==>[one()]

    Live proof. A lst of arrows composes as a relation, and the parametric one is its unit.

    mtron> [(a=>2),(b=>3)].mult([(2=>x),(3=>y)])
==>[a=>x,b=>y]
mtron> [(a=>2)].mult([one()])
==>[a=>2]

    Conclusion

    A ring of data need not hold its identities as values. When the unity a ring needs is an infinite value, such as the diagonal relation, the unity of the function ring stands for it. The arrow \(1 \Rightarrow 1\) is the diagonal written as one finite term, and composition evaluates it at one object per arrow, where it is needed (Theorem 2). The unity is parametric, so the one term is the unity at every type, and parametricity, not a check at each type, guarantees it (Theorem 3). The same compression gives lsts a unit. The slot product needs the one of every slot type, a family that no value holds (Theorem 4), and the lst \([\mathrm{one}]\) supplies it, its instruction becoming the one of whatever slot it meets (Theorem 5): a unit parametric in its operand rather than in its type, as the methods of a type class are. The same compression applies wherever an identity is a family indexed by infinitely many objects or types: the identity rewrite of a term rewriting system, the always-true filter of each type, and the identity morphism of each object of a category.

    NOTES:

    • mtron composes only the identity arrow by evaluation. Any other arrow between instructions composes by test, as an arrow between values does. Read as Definition 2 reads \(1 \Rightarrow 1\), the arrow \(1 \Rightarrow b\) is the graph of \(b\), and the collapse in the proof of Theorem 2 gives \((x \Rightarrow y) \cdot (1 \Rightarrow b) = x \Rightarrow b (y)\): composing with a graph evaluates its function at the one object the arrow meets. Should a rel between instructions compose as its graph?
    • As a stream function of type \(\forall A.\, A \to A^*\), its free theorem, with the one-object type of Theorem 3, gives \(f_A (x) = \langle x, \ldots, x \rangle\), with the same number \(n\) of copies at every type. So the parametric stream functions are the points \(n1\) of the unity [2]. The zero is \(n = 0\), so both identities of the function ring are points of its unity.
