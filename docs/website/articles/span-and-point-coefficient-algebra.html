    • A Span and Point Coefficient Algebra for Stream Rings

    An algebra concerning functions and objects where a point has magnitude and a span has a linear extension in space.
    Dr. Marko A. Rodriguez
    Dr. Stynx

    DRAFT: This article is currently being developed. As of November 10, 2026, the algebra has been fully specified, but the entailments of the presented theory have yet to be sufficiently explored.

    Introduction

    Stream Ring Theory [1] gave every function a coefficient. A term \(ca\) of the stream ring \(\langle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}, +, \cdot \rangle\) pairs a coefficient \(c \in \mathcal{C}\) with a function \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), and the coefficient floats: \(ca = a \cdot c\). This is called the point coefficient and it denotes the magnitude of the function. Stream ring theory finds practical application in its realization in the mtron programming language (the language of metatron). In mtron the point coefficient is written after its corresponding instruction, plus{3}(1) and denotes three parallel evaluations of the +1.

    The position in front of the instruction holds a second coefficient, the span: {3}plus(1) is semantically equivalent to plus(1).plus(1).plus(1), three steps laid one after another. The two coefficients form a compression of the two ring operations of stream ring theory: branch (\(+\)) and compose (\(\cdot\)). For a natural number \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), the point counts additions, \(na = a + \cdots + a\), and the span counts multiplications, \(a^n = a \cdot \cdots \cdot a\). A point can be understood as the mass at a single memory address. The span is the extent along a sequential series of addresses.

    Stream ring theory holds the bridge between them: the third equality of Theorem 6: \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\). In mtron, {n}a{c} is \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\): the span exponentiates the point.

    Live proof. Two steps of plus{3}(34) from 1: the value climbs to 69, and the point squares to 9. Thus, \((3a)^2\) and

    mtron> 1.{2}plus{3}(34)
==>{9}69

    In branch normal form, the point expanded, \(3a = a + a + a\), so \((3a)^2 = (a + a + a)^2\):

    mtron> 1.{2}{plus(34),plus(34),plus(34)}
==>{9}69

    In compose normal form, the span expanded, \((3a)^2 = 3a \cdot 3a\):

    mtron> 1.plus{3}(34).plus{3}(34)
==>{9}69

    Finally, in both compose and branch normal form, the sum of the \(3^2\) words \(a \cdot a\):

    mtron> 1.{plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34)}
==>{9}69

    The purpose of this article is to prove that the laws of each coefficient alone and of the two together, can be derived solely from the stream ring axioms of [1] as well as to demonstrate practical, utilitarian consequences of the interplay of these two coefficients with respect to data compression, process compression, co-located vs. distributed data structures, implications for compiler rewrite engines, and their role in mechanical induction.

    Two Coefficients

    The objects, coefficients and functions are those of [1]: \(x, y, z \in X\) are objects, \(c, d, e \in \mathcal{C}\) are coefficients of a ring with unity, and \(a, b, f \in \mathcal{F}\) are stream functions. The function ring has the zero \(0 (x) = \emptyset\) and the unity \(1 (x) = x\), and a stream is written \(\langle x, y, z \rangle\), with \(\langle x \rangle a\) the stream \(a\) outputs on \(x\). The proofs below cite the stream ring axioms of [1] by name: the apply, split, merge and bulk axioms, \(\langle 0x \rangle = \langle\rangle\), and the stream multiplication \(ca \cdot db = (c \cdot d)(a \cdot b)\).

    ::: Definition 1 (Point). The point of a term \(ca \in \mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\) is its coefficient \(c \in \mathcal{C}\). In mtron it is written after the instruction, a{c}. If \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then by the stream ring's addition \[ \begin{equation*} na = \underbrace{1a + \cdots + 1a}_{n}. \end{equation*} \] :::

    ::: Definition 2 (Span). The span of a term is the exponent \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) of its run, where \[ \begin{equation*} a^0 = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad a^{n+1} = a^n \cdot a. \end{equation*} \] In mtron it is written before the instruction, {n}a, and the parser lays the run out: {3}a is a.a.a, and {0}a is no instruction at all. A term with both coefficients, {n}a{c}, is \((ca)^n\). :::

    An mtron chain is read as a stream ring expression:

    mtronstream ring
    x.a\(\langle x \rangle a\)
    a.b\(a \cdot b\)
    branch(a,b)\(a + b\)
    id()\(1\)
    noobj\(0\)
    {c}x\(cx\)
    a{c}\(ca\)
    {n}a\(a^n\)
    {n}a{c}\((ca)^n\)

    Where a coefficient is written depends on what it is written on:

    written onprefixpostfix
    an instructionspan: {n}a is \(a^n\)point: a{c} is \(ca\)
    a typespan: {n}T is \(n\) slots of Tpoint: T{c} is \(c\) copies of T
    a valuepoint: {c}x is \(cx\)span: x{n} lays x along \(n\) slots

    The point coefficient can be understood as the mass at an address, copies compressed into a single point in space. The span coefficient can be understood as the extent along a contiguous sequence of points in space. In this way, \(+\) is the 0-dimensional operation and \(\cdot\) the 1-dimensional operation, which leads to the realization of the point iterating \(+\) and the span iterating \(\cdot\). In mtron, extension of instructions is function composition (a.b.c), where the locality of composition is in a uri address space. For example, plus(1).plus(2) compiles to plus(1)@<0>.plus(2)@<1>, where <0> and <1> are relative uri addresses in the virtual machine's variable stack. It's important to realize that composition is not application. A span lays one instruction along the axis of sequential addresses such that: {3}plus(1) is plus(1)@<0>.plus(1)@<1>.plus(1)@<2>.

    NOTES:

    • The mirror: instructions and types write {span}x{point}, values write {point}value{span}. {3}2 is three 2s at one address; 2{3} is 2 laid at the uris 0, 1 and 2. Why does the value side flip? (My guess, not settled: diagrammatic order, a value being where a chain starts.)

    The Point Coefficient

    ::: Theorem 1 (Points add across a branch). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(c,d \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(ca + da = (c+d)a\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(a (x) = y\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} ca + da &=& (c+d)a && \\ \langle x \rangle (ca + da) &=& \langle x \rangle (c+d)a && [\text{apply $1x$}] \\ (\langle x \rangle ca) + (\langle x \rangle da) &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{split, apply axioms}] \\ \langle cy \rangle + \langle dy \rangle &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}] \\ \langle cy, dy \rangle &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{merge axiom}] \\ \langle (c+d)y \rangle &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{bulk axiom}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 2 (Points multiply along a chain). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(c,d \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(ca \cdot db = (c \cdot d)(a \cdot b)\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(a (x) = y\) and \(b (y) = z\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} ca \cdot db &=& (c \cdot d)ab && \\ \langle x \rangle ca \cdot db &=& \langle x \rangle (c \cdot d)ab && [\text{apply $1x$}] \\ \langle cy \rangle db &=& \langle (c \cdot d)z \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}] \\ \langle (c \cdot d)z \rangle &=& \langle (c \cdot d)z \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 3 (Points are central). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\setminus \mathcal{F}_r\) and \(c \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(ca \cdot b = a \cdot cb = (ab)c\). :::

    ::: Proof. \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} ca \cdot b &=& ca \cdot 1b && [b = 1b] \\ &=& (c \cdot 1)(a \cdot b) && [\text{stream mult}] \\ &=& (1 \cdot c)(a \cdot b) && [\text{$1$ is unity in $\mathcal{C}$}] \\ &=& 1a \cdot cb && [\text{stream mult}] \\ &=& a \cdot cb && [a = 1a]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] The coefficient of \(c(ab)\) floats to its end, \(c(ab) = (ab)c\), by Theorem 6 of [1]. A reducer \(r \in \mathcal{F}_r\) is the exception: a coefficient does not float past it (Theorem 17 of [1]). ◻ :::

    Live proof.

    mtron> 5.branch(id{2}(),id{3}())

    ::: Theorem 4 (A zero point annihilates). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(0a = 0\). :::

    ::: Proof. The zero of \(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\) is \((0,0) \in \mathcal{C}\times \mathcal{F}\) [1]. If \(a (x) = y\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} 0a &=& 0 && \\ \langle x \rangle 0a &=& \langle x \rangle 0 && [\text{apply $1x$}] \\ \langle (1 \cdot 0)y \rangle &=& \langle 0\emptyset \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}] \\ \langle\rangle &=& \langle\rangle && [\langle 0x \rangle = \langle 0\emptyset \rangle = \langle\rangle]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    So a zero point ends the chain it is written in: \(0a \cdot b = 0 \cdot b = 0\), since no object reaches \(b\). A reducer \(r \in \mathcal{F}_r\) still runs, on the empty stream, by the reduce apply axiom of [1]: \(\langle x \rangle 0a \cdot r = \langle\rangle r = \langle r (\langle\rangle) \rangle\). For count(), \(r (\langle\rangle) = 0\).

    Live proof. A zero point leaves nothing for the rest of the chain. A branch adds it as it adds any point, and a reducer after it counts nothing.

    mtron> 5.id{0}()

mtron> 5.branch(id{0}(),id())
==>5
mtron> 5.id{0}().count()
==>0

    The Span Coefficient

    ::: Theorem 5 (Spans add along a chain). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(m,n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\). :::

    ::: Proof. By induction on \(n\). \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} a^m \cdot a^0 &=& a^m \cdot 1 = a^m && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ a^m \cdot a^{n+1} &=& a^m \cdot (a^n \cdot a) && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ &=& (a^m \cdot a^n) \cdot a && [\text{$\cdot$ is associative}] \\ &=& a^{m+n} \cdot a && [\text{induction}] \\ &=& a^{m+n+1} && [\text{Definition 2}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 6 (Spans stay where they are written). In general, \(a^n \cdot b \neq a \cdot b^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. A counterexample. If \(a (x) = x + 1\) and \(b (x) = 2x\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \langle 5 \rangle a^2 b = \langle 14 \rangle \neq \langle 24 \rangle = \langle 5 \rangle a b^2. \end{equation*} \] A point floats (Theorem 3), but a span is an exponent on its own function, and the monoid \(\langle\mathcal{F}, \cdot \rangle\) is not commutative. ◻ :::

    Live proof.

    mtron> 5.{2}plus(1).{3}plus(1)
==>10
mtron> 5.{0}plus(1)
==>5
mtron> 5.{2}plus(1).mult(2)
==>14
mtron> 5.plus(1).{2}mult(2)
==>24

    ::: Corollary 1 (Inductive generation). If \(a (x) = x + 1\), then \(\langle 0 \rangle a^n = \langle n \rangle\). :::

    ::: Proof. By induction on \(n\): \(\langle 0 \rangle a^0 = \langle 0 \rangle 1 = \langle 0 \rangle\), and \(\langle 0 \rangle a^{n+1} = \langle 0 \rangle a^n \cdot a = \langle n \rangle a = \langle n + 1 \rangle\). ◻ :::

    mtron> 0.{5}plus(1)
==>5

    ::: Corollary 2 (A constant's run is the constant). If \(k (x) = v\) for every object \(x\), then \(k \cdot k = k\), so \(k^n = k\) for every \(n \geq 1\), and \(k^0 = 1\). :::

    ::: Proof. \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} \langle x \rangle k \cdot k &=& \langle v \rangle k && [k (x) = v] \\ &=& \langle v \rangle && [k (v) = v] \\ &=& \langle x \rangle k && [k (x) = v]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] Then \(k^{n+1} = k^n \cdot k = k \cdot k = k\) by induction, and \(k^0 = 1\) by Definition 2. ◻ :::

    Live proof. A dereference reads its address, never its input, so *a is such a \(k\). Read left to right and greedy, {2}*a is {2}from(a), the run from(a).from(a).

    mtron> a -> 7
==>7
mtron> {2}*a
==>7
mtron> *a.*a
==>7

    A span on an object lays it out in space: \(n\) copies of \(x\) at the addresses \(0, 1, \ldots, n-1\), each copy keeping the point of \(x\). In mtron a value writes its span after it, x{n}, and until an address can be a branched uri the layout is the list of the copies, whose slots are those addresses: 2{3} is [2,2,2].

    mtron> 2{3}
==>2{3}
mtron> {2}2{3}
==>{2}2{3}

    NOTES: spans on a value. Once branched uris read and write a layout, is 2{3} the one value 2@<{0,1,2}> rather than the lst [2,2,2]? And is a laid value one obj or \(n\)? A ranged value span, 2{?}, has no layout yet and does not parse. Would 2{2,3} be the sum of its layouts, as a span range on an instruction is?

    The Algebra Relating Point and Span Coefficients

    ::: Theorem 7 (The span exponentiates the point). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), \(c \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. This is the third equality of Theorem 6 of [1]. By induction on \(n\): \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (ca)^0 &=& 1 = (1,1) = c^0 a^0 && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ (ca)^{n+1} &=& (ca)^n \cdot ca && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ &=& c^n a^n \cdot ca && [\text{induction}] \\ &=& (c^n \cdot c)(a^n \cdot a) && [\text{stream mult}] \\ &=& c^{n+1} a^{n+1} && [\text{Definition 2}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 3 (Order matters). A point on a run is not a run of the point: \(a^n \cdot c1 = c\,a^n\), while \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. \(a^n \cdot c1 = (1 \cdot c)(a^n \cdot 1) = c\,a^n\) by stream multiplication, and \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\) by Theorem 7. The two agree only where \(c^n = c\). ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 4 (Idempotents collapse). If \(e \cdot e = e\), then \((ce)^n = c^n e\) for every \(n \geq 1\). :::

    ::: Proof. \(e^n = e\) for \(n \geq 1\), by the induction of Corollary 2, so \((ce)^n = c^n e^n = c^n e\) by Theorem 7. The unity is idempotent, so \((c1)^n = c^n 1\). ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 5 (\(0^0 = 1\)). \((0a)^0 = 1\): a zero run of the annihilator is no step at all. :::

    ::: Proof. \(t^0 = 1\) for every term \(t\) by Definition 2, and \(0a = 0\) is a term (Theorem 4). ◻ :::

    Live proof.

    mtron> 5.{2}id{3}()
==>{9}5
mtron> 5.{2}id().id{3}()
==>{3}5
mtron> 5.{3}id{2}()
==>{8}5
mtron> 5.{0}id{0}()

    ::: Theorem 8 (A span does not distribute over a branch). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\), then \[ \begin{equation*} (a + b)^n = \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^n} w, \end{equation*} \] the sum of all \(2^n\) words of length \(n\) over \(a\) and \(b\). If \(ab = ba\), then \[ \begin{equation*} (a + b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k}, \end{equation*} \] and each binomial coefficient is a point. In general, \((a + b)^n \neq a^n + b^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. By induction on \(n\), expanding as [1] expands a multinomial: \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (a + b)^{n+1} &=& (a + b)^n \cdot (a + b) && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ &=& \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^n} w \cdot (a + b) && [\text{induction}] \\ &=& \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^n} (wa + wb) && [\text{$\cdot$ is left distributive}] \\ &=& \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^{n+1}} w && [\text{$+$ is associative}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] If \(ab = ba\), then a word with \(k\) copies of \(a\) is \(a^k b^{n-k}\), and there are \(\binom{n}{k}\) such words. Their unit points add to the point \(\binom{n}{k}\) by Theorem 1. ◻ :::

    Live proof. If \(a (x) = x + 1\), then the points of \((a + 1)^4\) are a row of Pascal's triangle: \(\langle 0 \rangle (a + 1)^4 = \langle 4, (4)3, (6)2, (4)1, 0 \rangle\). If \(b (x) = 2x\), then \(ab \neq ba\), and all four words of \((a + b)^2\) survive: \(\langle 5 \rangle (a + b)^2 = \langle 7, 12, 11, 20 \rangle\).

    mtron> 2.{2}branch(plus(1),id())

==>2
mtron> 0.{4}branch(plus(1),id())

==>{4}3

    ::: Theorem 9 (A shared prefix factors). If \(b,c \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(a \in \mathcal{F}_m \cup \mathcal{F}_f \cup \mathcal{F}_{fm}\) is pure, then \(a \cdot b + a \cdot c = a \cdot (b + c)\), and for \(m \leq n\), \[ \begin{equation*} a^m \cdot b + a^n \cdot c = a^m \cdot (b + a^{n-m} \cdot c). \end{equation*} \] :::

    ::: Proof. The first equality is the left distributivity of the function ring [1]. The second follows from it and Theorem 5, as \(a^n = a^m \cdot a^{n-m}\). The prefix must be pure: on the right it runs once for both members, so an effectful \(a\), a write or a print, would change what the expression does. It must also take one object at a time, a map, filter or flatmap: an mtron branch feeds its members one object at a time, so a reducer heading both members reads one object in each, but the whole stream once it is shared. (A distribution rewrite may state {*} on the branch it emits, making the shipment whole; that is the plan's optimization, not the instruction's default.) A common suffix factors the other way, \(b \cdot a + c \cdot a = (b + c) \cdot a\), by right distributivity, but never a reducer \(r \in \mathcal{F}_r\): the reduce near-ring is not right distributive [1]. ◻ :::

    Live proof. Two parallel expressions are one expression branched where their instructions differ. Runs of plus(1) under the spans 2 and 3 share the shorter run; a shared suffix merges after the branch; a shared reducer does not merge after it, and does not factor ahead of it. The cat instruction set's branch_factor rewrite applies the first equality as a normal form.

A reducer member is where the two readings part: is the per-object branch the intended \(+\)? The rewrites agree with the branch as it runs: branch_factor shares only a map, filter or flatmap. A distribution rewrite may state {*} on the branch it emits (the whole stream per member); the instruction's own default stays per-object.

    Coefficient Ranges

    A coefficient may be a range \([m,n]\) with \(m \leq n\). In mtron it is written {m,n}, and {?} is \([0,1]\), {+} is \([1,\infty)\) and {*} is \([0,\infty)\).

    ::: Definition 3 (Span range). If \(m \leq n\), then \[ \begin{equation*} a^{[m,n]} = \sum_{k=m}^{n} a^k = a^m + a^{m+1} + \cdots + a^n. \end{equation*} \] :::

    The unrolls \(a^k\) are independent terms, and each yields its own results. By the split axiom, \[ \begin{equation*} \langle x \rangle a^{[m,n]} = \sum_{k=m}^{n} \langle x \rangle a^k, \end{equation*} \] so paths of different lengths meet only in that sum, at the end of every path. A range is no uncertainty over its unrolls: every unroll is taken, and the multiplicities are not weights that sum to one. The zero-step unroll is the unity, \(a^0 = 1\), which passes its object on.

    ::: Corollary 6 (Each unroll carries its own power of the point). If \(c \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(m \leq n\), then \((ca)^{[m,n]} = \sum_{k=m}^{n} c^k a^k\). :::

    ::: Proof. By Definition 3, \((ca)^{[m,n]} = \sum_{k=m}^{n} (ca)^k\), and \((ca)^k = c^k a^k\) by Theorem 7. ◻ :::

    In mtron, {2,3}a answers what the branch of its unrolls, branch(a.a,a.a.a), answers, and {?}a what branch(id(),a) answers. The sum is computed when the instruction applies and is never written into the code: a path ends where its monad's instruction pointer reaches noobj, and the objects of the halted monads sum there. A monad whose object is noobj is killed instead, the zero point of Theorem 4. As the code keeps the span, code read as a pattern still matches {?}neg() as a run of none or one neg().

    Live proof. If \(a (x) = x + 1\), then \(\langle 1 \rangle a^{[2,3]} = \langle 1 \rangle (a^2 + a^3) = \langle 3, 4 \rangle\) and \(\langle 1 \rangle a^{[0,1]} = \langle 1, 2 \rangle\). By Corollary 6, \(\langle 1 \rangle (3a)^{[2,3]} = \langle (9)3, (27)4 \rangle\), and a multiplicity on the input counts once: \(\langle (2)1 \rangle (3a)^{[2,3]} = \langle (18)3, (54)4 \rangle\).

    A range on a point is a range of multiplicities: if \(b (x) = x + 2\), then \(\langle 1 \rangle\, [0,1]\, b = \langle ([0,1])3 \rangle\), a three held zero or one times.

    mtron> 1.plus{?}(2)
==>{?}3

    Generation. If \(a (x) = x + 1\), then a range from zero counts. By Definition 3 and Corollary 1, \[ \begin{equation*} \langle 0 \rangle a^{[0,n]} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \langle 0 \rangle a^k = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \langle k \rangle = \langle 0, 1, \ldots, n \rangle. \end{equation*} \] A point on the step weighs the natural \(k\) by its power, by Corollary 6: \[ \begin{equation*} \langle 0 \rangle (2a)^{[0,n]} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} 2^k \langle k \rangle = \langle 0, (2)1, (4)2, \ldots, (2^n)n \rangle, \end{equation*} \] a stream of \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} 2^k = 2^{n+1} - 1\) objects. The weights count paths: \(2a = a + a\) by Definition 1, so \((2a)^k = (a + a)^k\) is the sum of the \(2^k\) words of length \(k\) over two copies of \(a\) (Theorem 8), and every one of them lands on \(k\).

    Live proof. With \(n = 6\): the naturals to six, then the same seven values weighted \(1, 2, 4, \ldots, 64\), which is \(2^7 - 1 = 127\) objects.

    An open range is the Kleene star [3], \(a^{[0,\infty)} = a^* = 1 + a + a^2 + \cdots\). If \(a (x) = x + 1\), then \(\langle 0 \rangle a^* = \langle 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots \rangle\): every natural at once, the bounded generations above being its prefixes. A span range is a generator. In mtron it is 0.{*}plus(1), which is start(0).branch(id(),plus(1),plus(1).plus(1),...).

    NOTES: an open range has no last unroll, so 0.{*}plus(1) (now {*}1) needs its unrolls on demand, \(a^* = 1 + a \cdot a^*\); repeat already runs the bounded form. To settle here: how a point range such as {?}3 reads now that a range is no uncertainty, and the type of a generator.

So the coefficient of the inferred type is \(c^n\): type() reads the point of a term off its run, and cc() reads the coefficient of that type. In the console, 5.{2}plus{3}(1).type() is int{9}::T.
