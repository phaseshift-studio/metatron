    • A Span and Point Coefficient Algebra for Stream Rings

    An algebra concerning functions and objects where a point has magnitude and a span has a linear extension in space.
    Dr. Marko A. Rodriguez
    Dr. Stynx

    DRAFT: This article is currently being developed. As of November 10, 2026, the algebra has been fully specified, but the entailments of the presented theory have yet to be sufficiently explored.

    Introduction

    Stream Ring Theory [1] gave every function a coefficient. A term \(ca\) of the stream ring \(\langle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}, +, \cdot \rangle\) pairs a coefficient \(c \in \mathcal{C}\) with a function \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), and the coefficient floats: \(ca = a \cdot c\). This is called the point coefficient and it denotes the magnitude of the function. Stream ring theory finds practical application in its realization in the mtron programming language (the language of metatron). In mtron the point coefficient is written after its corresponding instruction, plus{3}(1) and denotes three parallel evaluations of the +1.

    The position in front of the instruction holds a second coefficient, the span: {3}plus(1) is semantically equivalent to plus(1).plus(1).plus(1), three steps laid one after another. The two coefficients form a compression of the two ring operations of stream ring theory: branch (\(+\)) and compose (\(\cdot\)). For a natural number \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), the point counts additions, \(na = a + \cdots + a\), and the span counts multiplications, \(a^n = a \cdot \cdots \cdot a\). A point can be understood as the mass at a single memory address. The span is the extent along a sequential series of addresses.

    Stream ring theory holds the bridge between them: the third equality of Theorem 6: \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\). A run of the pointed term exponentiates its point.

    Live proof. Two steps of plus{3}(34) from 1: the value climbs to 69, and the point squares to 9. Thus, \((3a)^2\) and

    mtron> 1.plus{3}(34).plus{3}(34)
==>{2}69

    In branch normal form, the point expanded, \(3a = a + a + a\), so \((3a)^2 = (a + a + a)^2\):

    mtron> 1.{2}{plus(34),plus(34),plus(34)}
==>{9}69

    Finally, in both compose and branch normal form, the sum of the \(3^2\) words \(a \cdot a\):

    mtron> 1.{plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34),
          plus(34).plus(34)}
==>{2}69

    Written on one instruction, the two coefficients are its two extents: {2}plus{3}(34) is three lanes of the run plus(34).plus(34), \(3a^2\), its point taken once, as map{3} takes it once for the run it maps:

    mtron> 1.{2}plus{3}(34)
==>{2}69
mtron> 1.plus(34).plus{3}(34)
==>{2}69
mtron> 1.map{3}(plus(34).plus(34))
==>{3}69

    The purpose of this article is to prove that the laws of each coefficient alone and of the two together, can be derived solely from the stream ring axioms of [1] as well as to demonstrate practical, utilitarian consequences of the interplay of these two coefficients with respect to data compression, process compression, co-located vs. distributed data structures, implications for compiler rewrite engines, and their role in mechanical induction.

    Two Coefficients

    The objects, coefficients and functions are those of [1]: \(x, y, z \in X\) are objects, \(c, d, e \in \mathcal{C}\) are coefficients of a ring with unity, and \(a, b, f \in \mathcal{F}\) are stream functions. The function ring has the zero \(0 (x) = \emptyset\) and the unity \(1 (x) = x\), and a stream is written \(\langle x, y, z \rangle\), with \(\langle x \rangle a\) the stream \(a\) outputs on \(x\). The proofs below cite the stream ring axioms of [1] by name: the apply, split, merge and bulk axioms, \(\langle 0x \rangle = \langle\rangle\), and the stream multiplication \(ca \cdot db = (c \cdot d)(a \cdot b)\).

    ::: Definition 1 (Point). The point of a term \(ca \in \mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\) is its coefficient \(c \in \mathcal{C}\). In mtron it is written after the instruction, a{c}. If \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then by the stream ring's addition \[ \begin{equation*} na = \underbrace{1a + \cdots + 1a}_{n}. \end{equation*} \] :::

    ::: Definition 2 (Span). The span of a term is the exponent \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) of its run, where \[ \begin{equation*} a^0 = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad a^{n+1} = a^n \cdot a. \end{equation*} \] In mtron it is written before the instruction, {n}a, and stays on it: {3}a is one instruction whose run a.a.a is walked when it applies, never written out into the code, and {0}a is \(a^0 = 1\), the identity id(). A term with both coefficients, {n}a{c}, is \(c\) lanes of its run, \(c\,a^n\): the point is parallel and the span serial, so the point is taken once by the run, and {0}a{c} is id{c}(). :::

    An mtron chain is read as a stream ring expression:

    mtronstream ring
    x.a\(\langle x \rangle a\)
    a.b\(a \cdot b\)
    branch(a,b)\(a + b\)
    id()\(1\)
    noobj\(0\)
    {c}x\(cx\)
    a{c}\(ca\)
    {n}a\(a^n\)
    {n}a{c}\(c\,a^n\)
    {-n}a\((a^{\smile})^n\)

    Where a coefficient is written depends on what it is written on:

    written onprefixpostfix
    an instructionspan: {n}a is \(a^n\)point: a{c} is \(ca\)
    a typespan: {n}T is \(T^n = T\), with \(T^0 = 1\)point: T{c} is \(c\) copies of T
    a valuepoint: {c}x is \(cx\)span: x{n} lays x along \(n\) slots
    a bracket entrypoint: {c}T is \(c\) objects at one slotspan: T{n} is \(n\) slots of T

    The point coefficient can be understood as the mass at an address, copies compressed into a single point in space. The span coefficient can be understood as the extent along a contiguous sequence of points in space. In this way, \(+\) is the 0-dimensional operation and \(\cdot\) the 1-dimensional operation, which leads to the realization of the point iterating \(+\) and the span iterating \(\cdot\). In mtron, extension of instructions is function composition (a.b.c), where the locality of composition is in a uri address space. For example, plus(1).plus(2) compiles to plus(1)@<0>.plus(2)@<1>, where <0> and <1> are relative uri addresses in the virtual machine's variable stack. It's important to realize that composition is not application. A span lays one instruction along the axis of sequential addresses, so that {3}plus(1) means plus(1)@<0>.plus(1)@<1>.plus(1)@<2>, and it compresses that axis as the point compresses copies: the compiled code holds the one instruction {3}plus(1)@<0>, whose run is walked when it applies (Theorem 10).

    The Point Coefficient

    ::: Theorem 1 (Points add across a branch). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(c,d \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(ca + da = (c+d)a\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(a (x) = y\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} ca + da &=& (c+d)a && \\ \langle x \rangle (ca + da) &=& \langle x \rangle (c+d)a && [\text{apply $1x$}] \\ (\langle x \rangle ca) + (\langle x \rangle da) &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{split, apply axioms}] \\ \langle cy \rangle + \langle dy \rangle &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}] \\ \langle cy, dy \rangle &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{merge axiom}] \\ \langle (c+d)y \rangle &=& \langle (c+d)y \rangle && [\text{bulk axiom}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 2 (Points multiply along a chain). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(c,d \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(ca \cdot db = (c \cdot d)(a \cdot b)\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(a (x) = y\) and \(b (y) = z\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} ca \cdot db &=& (c \cdot d)ab && \\ \langle x \rangle ca \cdot db &=& \langle x \rangle (c \cdot d)ab && [\text{apply $1x$}] \\ \langle cy \rangle db &=& \langle (c \cdot d)z \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}] \\ \langle (c \cdot d)z \rangle &=& \langle (c \cdot d)z \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 3 (Points are central). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\setminus \mathcal{F}_r\) and \(c \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(ca \cdot b = a \cdot cb = (ab)c\). :::

    ::: Proof. \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} ca \cdot b &=& ca \cdot 1b && [b = 1b] \\ &=& (c \cdot 1)(a \cdot b) && [\text{stream mult}] \\ &=& (1 \cdot c)(a \cdot b) && [\text{$1$ is unity in $\mathcal{C}$}] \\ &=& 1a \cdot cb && [\text{stream mult}] \\ &=& a \cdot cb && [a = 1a]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] The coefficient of \(c(ab)\) floats to its end, \(c(ab) = (ab)c\), by Theorem 6 of [1]. A reducer \(r \in \mathcal{F}_r\) is the exception: a coefficient does not float past it (Theorem 17 of [1]). ◻ :::

    Live proof.

    ::: Theorem 4 (A zero point annihilates). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(0a = 0\). :::

    ::: Proof. The zero of \(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\) is \((0,0) \in \mathcal{C}\times \mathcal{F}\) [1]. If \(a (x) = y\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} 0a &=& 0 && \\ \langle x \rangle 0a &=& \langle x \rangle 0 && [\text{apply $1x$}] \\ \langle (1 \cdot 0)y \rangle &=& \langle 0\emptyset \rangle && [\text{apply axiom}] \\ \langle\rangle &=& \langle\rangle && [\langle 0x \rangle = \langle 0\emptyset \rangle = \langle\rangle]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    So a zero point ends the chain it is written in: \(0a \cdot b = 0 \cdot b = 0\), since no object reaches \(b\). A reducer \(r \in \mathcal{F}_r\) still runs, on the empty stream, by the reduce apply axiom of [1]: \(\langle x \rangle 0a \cdot r = \langle\rangle r = \langle r (\langle\rangle) \rangle\). For count(), \(r (\langle\rangle) = 0\).

    Live proof. A zero point leaves nothing for the rest of the chain. A branch adds it as it adds any point, and a reducer after it counts nothing.

    mtron> 5.id{0}()
mtron> 5.plus{0}(1).plus(1)
mtron> 5.branch(id{0}(),id())
==>5
mtron> 5.id{0}().count()
==>0

    The Span Coefficient

    ::: Theorem 5 (Spans add along a chain). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(m,n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\). :::

    ::: Proof. By induction on \(n\). \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} a^m \cdot a^0 &=& a^m \cdot 1 = a^m && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ a^m \cdot a^{n+1} &=& a^m \cdot (a^n \cdot a) && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ &=& (a^m \cdot a^n) \cdot a && [\text{$\cdot$ is associative}] \\ &=& a^{m+n} \cdot a && [\text{induction}] \\ &=& a^{m+n+1} && [\text{Definition 2}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 6 (Spans stay where they are written). In general, \(a^n \cdot b \neq a \cdot b^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. A counterexample. If \(a (x) = x + 1\) and \(b (x) = 2x\), then \[ \begin{equation*} \langle 5 \rangle a^2 b = \langle 14 \rangle \neq \langle 24 \rangle = \langle 5 \rangle a b^2. \end{equation*} \] A point floats (Theorem 3), but a span is an exponent on its own function, and the monoid \(\langle\mathcal{F}, \cdot \rangle\) is not commutative. ◻ :::

    Live proof.

    mtron> 5.{2}plus(1).{3}plus(1)
==>10
mtron> 5.{0}plus(1)
==>5
mtron> 5.{2}plus(1).mult(2)
==>14
mtron> 5.plus(1).{2}mult(2)
==>24

    ::: Corollary 1 (Inductive generation). If \(a (x) = x + 1\), then \(\langle 0 \rangle a^n = \langle n \rangle\). :::

    ::: Proof. By induction on \(n\): \(\langle 0 \rangle a^0 = \langle 0 \rangle 1 = \langle 0 \rangle\), and \(\langle 0 \rangle a^{n+1} = \langle 0 \rangle a^n \cdot a = \langle n \rangle a = \langle n + 1 \rangle\). ◻ :::

    mtron> 0.{5}plus(1)
==>5

    ::: Corollary 2 (A constant's run is the constant). If \(k (x) = v\) for every object \(x\), then \(k \cdot k = k\), so \(k^n = k\) for every \(n \geq 1\), and \(k^0 = 1\). :::

    ::: Proof. \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} \langle x \rangle k \cdot k &=& \langle v \rangle k && [k (x) = v] \\ &=& \langle v \rangle && [k (v) = v] \\ &=& \langle x \rangle k && [k (x) = v]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] Then \(k^{n+1} = k^n \cdot k = k \cdot k = k\) by induction, and \(k^0 = 1\) by Definition 2. ◻ :::

    Live proof. A dereference reads its address, never its input, so *a is such a \(k\). Read left to right and greedy, {2}*a is {2}from(a), one instruction whose run is from(a).from(a).

    mtron> a -> 7
==>7

==>7

==>7

    A span on an object lays it out in space: \(n\) copies of \(x\) at the addresses \(0, 1, \ldots, n-1\), each copy keeping the point of \(x\), so \((c)x\) along \(n\) slots counts \(c \cdot n\) objects. In mtron a value writes its span after it, x{n}, and keeps it curled on its type: 2{3} is one 2 that counts three, and {3}5{3} counts nine and sums to 45. A space with an axis is what lays it out. A lst is such an axis, [3{4}] being [3,3,3,3], and so is an instruction's argument list, a lst too: 5.branch(6{2}) is 5.branch(6,6).

    mtron> 2{3}
==>2{3}
mtron> {2}2{3}
==>{2}2{3}
mtron> 2{3}.count()
==>3
mtron> {3}5{3}.count()
==>9
mtron> {3}5{3}.sum()
==>45
mtron> [3{4}]
==>[3,3,3,3]
mtron> 5.branch(6{2})
==>6{2}

    The Algebra Relating Point and Span Coefficients

    ::: Theorem 7 (The span exponentiates the point). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), \(c \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. This is the third equality of Theorem 6 of [1]. By induction on \(n\): \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (ca)^0 &=& 1 = (1,1) = c^0 a^0 && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ (ca)^{n+1} &=& (ca)^n \cdot ca && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ &=& c^n a^n \cdot ca && [\text{induction}] \\ &=& (c^n \cdot c)(a^n \cdot a) && [\text{stream mult}] \\ &=& c^{n+1} a^{n+1} && [\text{Definition 2}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 3 (Order matters). A point on a run is not a run of the point: \(a^n \cdot c1 = c\,a^n\), while \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. \(a^n \cdot c1 = (1 \cdot c)(a^n \cdot 1) = c\,a^n\) by stream multiplication, and \((ca)^n = c^n a^n\) by Theorem 7. The two agree only where \(c^n = c\). ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 4 (Idempotents collapse). If \(e \cdot e = e\), then \((ce)^n = c^n e\) for every \(n \geq 1\). :::

    ::: Proof. \(e^n = e\) for \(n \geq 1\), by the induction of Corollary 2, so \((ce)^n = c^n e^n = c^n e\) by Theorem 7. The unity is idempotent, so \((c1)^n = c^n 1\). ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 5 (\(0^0 = 1\)). \((0a)^0 = 1\): a zero run of the annihilator is the unity, not the zero. :::

    ::: Proof. \(t^0 = 1\) for every term \(t\) by Definition 2, and \(0a = 0\) is a term (Theorem 4). ◻ :::

    So a zero point annihilates only a step that runs: \((0a)^n = 0^n a^n\) is \(0\) for \(n \geq 1\) and \(1\) for \(n = 0\) (Theorem 7), and a span range, a sum of such runs (Definition 3, below), is \(0\) only if it holds no zero-step run. In mtron, {n}a{c} is \(c\,a^n\), the point on the run (Corollary 3), so {0}a{c} is id{c}() and an instruction is noobj exactly when its point is zero. So is a type, which as a step is a filter: {0}int{3}::T runs its filter no times, so it takes every object three times, while int{0}::T is noobj. The run of the pointed term, \((ca)^n\), is the pointed step written out, or a span on the branch its point stands for, so \((0a)^0 = 1\) is a zero span on the branch of no members. Tested by the product \(c \cdot n\), which is the count of a value's copies, the zero span read as the zero: a branch member {0}a dropped its object, \(\langle 5 \rangle (a^0 + b)\) answering \(\langle 5 \rangle b\) alone.

    ::: Theorem 8 (A span does not distribute over a branch). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\), then \[ \begin{equation*} (a + b)^n = \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^n} w, \end{equation*} \] the sum of all \(2^n\) words of length \(n\) over \(a\) and \(b\). If \(ab = ba\), then \[ \begin{equation*} (a + b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k}, \end{equation*} \] and each binomial coefficient is a point. In general, \((a + b)^n \neq a^n + b^n\). :::

    ::: Proof. By induction on \(n\), expanding as [1] expands a multinomial: \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (a + b)^{n+1} &=& (a + b)^n \cdot (a + b) && [\text{Definition 2}] \\ &=& \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^n} w \cdot (a + b) && [\text{induction}] \\ &=& \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^n} (wa + wb) && [\text{$\cdot$ is left distributive}] \\ &=& \sum_{w \in \{a,b\}^{n+1}} w && [\text{$+$ is associative}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] If \(ab = ba\), then a word with \(k\) copies of \(a\) is \(a^k b^{n-k}\), and there are \(\binom{n}{k}\) such words. Their unit points add to the point \(\binom{n}{k}\) by Theorem 1. ◻ :::

If \(b (x) = 2x\), then \(ab \neq ba\), and all four words of \((a + b)^2\) survive: \(\langle 5 \rangle (a + b)^2 = \langle 7, 12, 11, 20 \rangle\).

    ::: Theorem 9 (A shared prefix factors). If \(b,c \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(a \in \mathcal{F}_m \cup \mathcal{F}_f \cup \mathcal{F}_{fm}\) is pure, then \(a \cdot b + a \cdot c = a \cdot (b + c)\), and for \(m \leq n\), \[ \begin{equation*} a^m \cdot b + a^n \cdot c = a^m \cdot (b + a^{n-m} \cdot c). \end{equation*} \] :::

    ::: Proof. The first equality is the left distributivity of the function ring [1]. The second follows from it and Theorem 5, as \(a^n = a^m \cdot a^{n-m}\). The prefix must be pure: on the right it runs once for both members, so an effectful \(a\), a write or a print, would change what the expression does. It must also take one object at a time, a map, filter or flatmap: an mtron branch feeds its members one object at a time, so a reducer heading both members reads one object in each, but the whole stream once it is shared. (A distribution rewrite may state {*} on the branch it emits, making the shipment whole; that is the plan's optimization, not the instruction's default.)     Evaluating a Span

    Definition 3 sums independent unrolls, and the plain way to compute the sum takes them one at a time: the unroll \(a^k\) applies \(a\) to the input \(k\) times, so \(a^{[m,n]}\) costs \(\sum_{k=m}^{n} k\) applications, and an exact span written out as its compose chain \(a \cdot \cdots \cdot a\) is a term \(n\) functions long. The unrolls share their prefixes, and a shared prefix factors. Factored all at once, the sum is Horner's rule.

    ::: Theorem 10 (A span range is one walk). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) is pure and \(m \leq n\), then \[ \begin{equation*} a^{[m,n]} = a^m \cdot (1 + a \cdot (1 + a \cdot (\cdots (1 + a)))), \end{equation*} \] with \(n - m\) factors of \(a\) after \(a^m\). So if \(\langle x_0 \rangle = \langle x \rangle\) and \(\langle x_{k+1} \rangle = \langle x_k \rangle a\), then \(\langle x \rangle a^{[m,n]} = \sum_{k=m}^{n} \langle x_k \rangle\): each unroll is one step past the last. :::

    ::: Proof. A pure prefix factors on a stream whatever it does to the stream, by the split axiom: if \(\langle x \rangle a = \langle y \rangle\), then \(\langle x \rangle (a \cdot b + a \cdot c) = \langle y \rangle b + \langle y \rangle c = \langle y \rangle (b + c) = \langle x \rangle a \cdot (b + c)\). So \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} a^{[m,n]} &=& \sum_{k=m}^{n} a^m \cdot a^{k-m} && [\text{Definition 3, Theorem 5}] \\ &=& a^m \cdot \sum_{j=0}^{n-m} a^j = a^m \cdot a^{[0,n-m]} && [\text{shared prefix}] \\ a^{[0,j]} &=& 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{j} a \cdot a^{i-1} = 1 + a \cdot a^{[0,j-1]} && [\text{Definition 3, Theorem 5, shared prefix}], \end{array} \end{equation*} \] and unfolding \(a^{[0,j]}\) down to \(a^{[0,0]} = 1\) nests the \(n - m\) factors. ◻ :::

    Purity is the one condition, as in Theorem 9: the factored prefix runs once for every unroll it heads. The other condition of Theorem 9, that the prefix take one object at a time, does not arise. The sum of Definition 3 is the split axiom applied to the whole stream \(\langle x \rangle\), so a reducer heads its unrolls as any function does. It is mtron's branch, feeding its members one object at a time, that is not this sum.

    ::: Corollary 7 (The cost of a walk). A walk of \(a^{[m,n]}\) applies \(a\) \(n\) times, where the unrolls taken one at a time apply it \(\sum_{k=m}^{n} k = \frac{(n-m+1)(m+n)}{2}\) times. :::

    ::: Proof. Each \(\langle x_k \rangle\) is one application past \(\langle x_{k-1} \rangle\), for \(k = 1, \ldots, n\), and the unroll \(a^k\) alone applies \(a\) \(k\) times. ◻ :::

    ::: Corollary 8 (A walk halts where its run leaves the domain). Read \(a\) as partial on its domain \(D\), with \(\langle x \rangle a = \langle\rangle\) for \(x \notin D\).     ::: Proof. A function that is no reducer applies to a run object by object (the split axiom), each object outside \(D\) adding \(\langle\rangle\), and it maps the empty stream to the empty stream. ◻ :::

    Negative Spans: the Converse

    A span counts the steps of a run, and a negative span counts them backward. The backward step is not an inverse that cancels the forward one, as a function that sends two objects to one place cannot be undone. It is the function's converse: the relation the function states, read from its other end [4].

    Read a function as that relation, with multiplicities. If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), let \(\mu_a (x,y)\) be the coefficient of \(y\) in \(\langle x \rangle a\), so that \(\langle x \rangle a = \sum_y \mu_a (x,y)\, y\), each output once by the bulk axiom. By the apply, split and bulk axioms, composition multiplies these relations as matrices, \(\mu_{a \cdot b} (x,z) = \sum_y \mu_a (x,y)\, \mu_b (y,z)\); by the split and merge axioms, a branch adds them, \(\mu_{a + b} = \mu_a + \mu_b\); and a point scales them, \(\mu_{ca} = c\, \mu_a\).

    ::: Definition 4 (Converse). The converse \(a^{\smile}\) of \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) is the relation of \(a\) read from its other end, \(\mu_{a^{\smile}} (y,x) = \mu_a (x,y)\): \[ \begin{equation*} \langle y \rangle a^{\smile} = \sum_x \mu_a (x,y)\, x, \end{equation*} \] every object that \(a\) sends to \(y\), as often as \(a\) sends it there. :::

    The converse is a stream function only where each object has finitely many preimages. A constant has every object as a preimage of its value, and a count every stream of its size, so neither converse can be listed.

    ::: Definition 5 (Negative span). If \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \(a^{-n} = (a^{\smile})^n\), and a span range \(a^{[m,n]} = \sum_{k=m}^{n} a^k\) (Definition 3) may cross zero. In mtron a negative span is written {-n}a, and a range {-m,n}a. :::

    ::: Theorem 11 (The converse is an involution). If \(a,b \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(c \in \mathcal{C}\), with \(\mathcal{C}\) commutative, then \[ \begin{equation*} (a + b)^{\smile} = a^{\smile} + b^{\smile}, \quad (a \cdot b)^{\smile} = b^{\smile} \cdot a^{\smile}, \quad (ca)^{\smile} = c\, a^{\smile}, \quad (a^{\smile})^{\smile} = a, \quad 1^{\smile} = 1, \quad 0^{\smile} = 0. \end{equation*} \] :::

    ::: Proof. Each equality is one of the relations read from its other end: \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} \mu_{(a + b)^{\smile}} (y,x) &=& \mu_a (x,y) + \mu_b (x,y) = \mu_{a^{\smile} + b^{\smile}} (y,x) && [\mu_{a+b} = \mu_a + \mu_b] \\ \mu_{(a \cdot b)^{\smile}} (z,x) &=& \sum_y \mu_a (x,y)\, \mu_b (y,z) && [\text{composition}] \\ &=& \sum_y \mu_{b^{\smile}} (z,y)\, \mu_{a^{\smile}} (y,x) = \mu_{b^{\smile} \cdot a^{\smile}} (z,x) && [\text{$\mathcal{C}$ commutes, Definition 4}] \\ \mu_{(ca)^{\smile}} (y,x) &=& c\, \mu_a (x,y) = \mu_{c\, a^{\smile}} (y,x) && [\mu_{ca} = c\, \mu_a]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] Read twice from the other end, a relation reads from its first end again, and \(\mu_1 (x,y)\), which is \(1\) where \(x = y\) and \(0\) elsewhere, reads the same from both ends, as \(\mu_0 = 0\) does. ◻ :::

    So the stream ring with its converse is a ring with an involution, a \(*\)-ring. Over the complex numbers the involution conjugates the coefficient as well, \((ca)^{\smile} = \bar{c}\, a^{\smile}\), and the converse of a quantum walk is its adjoint: the conjugate transpose with which [1] inverts a wave. The adjoint of a unitary is its inverse.

    ::: Corollary 9 (A converse is no inverse). If \(a\) is a bijection, then \(a \cdot a^{\smile} = a^{\smile} \cdot a = 1\), and \(a^{-1}\) is the inverse of \(a\). In general \(a \cdot a^{\smile} \neq 1\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(a\) is a bijection, then \(\mu_a (x,y)\) is \(1\) where \(y = a (x)\) and \(0\) elsewhere, so \(\mu_{a \cdot a^{\smile}} (x,x') = \sum_y \mu_a (x,y)\, \mu_a (x',y)\) is \(1\) where \(a (x) = a (x')\), which is where \(x = x'\), as \(a\) is injective. That is \(\mu_1\), and the other order is the same with the bijection \(a^{\smile}\). If instead \(a\) sends two objects to one, \(a (x) = a (x') = y\) with \(x \neq x'\), then \(\langle x \rangle a \cdot a^{\smile} = \langle y \rangle a^{\smile} = \langle x, x' \rangle \neq \langle x \rangle\). ◻ :::

    The forward run and the backward run cancel only at a bijection, where the converse is the inverse. In a graph, out() read backward is in(), and out().in() reaches the siblings of a vertex, not the vertex alone.

    ::: Theorem 12 (Signed spans add when their signs agree). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(m,n \in \mathbb{Z}\) with \(mn \geq 0\), then \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\). :::

    ::: Proof. If \(m,n \geq 0\), this is Theorem 5. If \(m,n \leq 0\), then \(a^m \cdot a^n = (a^{\smile})^{-m} \cdot (a^{\smile})^{-n} = (a^{\smile})^{-m-n} = a^{m+n}\) by Definition 5 and Theorem 5 for \(a^{\smile}\). If \(m > 0 > n\), the law holds for a bijection and fails in general, as \(a \cdot a^{-1} \neq a^0\) (Corollary 9). ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 13 (A negative span keeps the power of the point). If \(a \in \mathcal{F}\), \(c \in \mathcal{C}\), with \(\mathcal{C}\) commutative, and \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \((ca)^{-n} = c^n a^{-n}\). :::

    ::: Proof. \[ \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lclll} (ca)^{-n} &=& ((ca)^{\smile})^n && [\text{Definition 5}] \\ &=& (c\, a^{\smile})^n && [\text{Theorem 11}] \\ &=& c^n (a^{\smile})^n = c^n a^{-n} && [\text{Theorem 7, Definition 5}]. \end{array} \end{equation*} \] ◻ :::

    The point does not invert: a run backward follows as many paths as the run forward, so Theorem 7 holds for every integer span, with the point to the power \(|n|\).

    ::: Corollary 10 (A range across zero walks both ways). If \(m,n \in \mathbb{N}\), then \(a^{[-m,n]} = (a^{\smile})^{[1,m]} + a^{[0,n]}\): from its input, the walk of Theorem 10 runs backward through \(a^{\smile}\) and forward through \(a\). :::

    ::: Proof. Definition 3, with the sum split at zero, and Definition 5. ◻ :::

    ::: Theorem 14 (A filter is its own converse). If \(f \in \mathcal{F}\) is a filter, \(\langle x \rangle f \in \{\langle x \rangle, \langle\rangle\}\), then \(f^{\smile} = f\). :::

    ::: Proof. \(\mu_f (x,y) = 0\) wherever \(y \neq x\), and a relation that holds only where \(x = y\) reads the same from both ends. ◻ :::

    Each coefficient carries its own sign. A negative point is the additive inverse: \(a + (-1)a = (1 - 1)a = 0\) by Theorems 1 and 4, so copies of opposite sign cancel. A negative span is the converse, which reverses and cancels only at a bijection. The point's sign belongs to \(+\) and the span's to \(\cdot\), as the coefficients themselves do.

    Live proof. Runs of one sign add, \(b^{-1} \cdot b^{-1} = b^{-2}\) (Theorem 12). Negation is its own converse, and a real doubling's converse is a halving.

    In mtron, an instruction declares its converse in its ?decq claim, as the contract it runs backward: plus?int<=int(int) names minus?int<=int(int), and neg names itself.     Point and Span Coefficients Type Systems

    If \(a (x) = y\), then \(\langle x \rangle c\,a^n = \langle c\,a^n (x) \rangle\) and \(\langle x \rangle (ca)^n = \langle c^n a^n (x) \rangle\) by Theorem 7 and the apply axiom. So the coefficient of the inferred type of {n}a{c} is \(c\), and of the run of the pointed step \(c^n\): type() reads the point of a term off its run, and cc() reads the coefficient of that type. In the console, 5.{2}plus{3}(1).type() is int{3}::T. The compiler types a span by its runs, never by its span times its point, which is the count of a value's copies ({3}5{3} counts nine): 0.{10}plus(4) is typed one int, not ten.

mtron> 5.plus{3}(1).plus{3}(1).type().cc()
==>2
mtron> 5.plus(1).id{3}().type().cc()
==>2

    A type is an object that performs a computation as well as one that is used in one, and which it is depends on where it stands. With no axis, as a step or as a term of a branch, a type is a filter: \(\langle x \rangle T = \langle x \rangle\) when \(x\) is a T, and \(\langle x \rangle T = 0\) otherwise. Its span makes code of it, {4}int::T being int::T.int::T.int::T.int::T, and a filter is idempotent, \(T \cdot T = T\), so the span of a type is the power of an idempotent, \(T^n = T\) for \(n \geq 1\) and \(T^0 = 1\) by Definition 2, while its point is a sum, \(cT = T + \cdots + T\), and makes copies. So {2}int{3}::T is int{3}::T, three parallel checks and never nine, and {0}int{3}::T is id{3}(), which takes every object three times.

    On an axis the same span is extension. A lst is an axis, and so is an argument list and a bracket, whose entries are uris, which are values, written as values are, {point}value{span}. There the span is a power in the container's own monoid, concatenation, whose unit is []. That monoid is free, not idempotent: \([T]^n = [T, \ldots, T]\), \(n\) slots. So lst[int{4}] is lst[int,int,int,int], runs add along the list (lst[int{2},int,int] is lst[int{4}]), and the point is the mass at one slot: lst[{4}int] is one slot holding four ints. The same holds for every object on an axis, a type and an instruction too: [{4}int::T] is [int::T,int::T,int::T,int::T], so 1-<[{4}int::T] is [1,1,1,1] while 1-<[int{4}::T] is [{4}1], and 1-<[{2}plus(1),{3}mult(2)] maps one object over a lst of instructions, the converse of a map. ? is isa, whose operand is an argument list, so 1?{4}int::T is 1.isa(int::T,int::T,int::T,int::T), which isa reads as 1.isa(int::T). The one notation {n} is a power in whichever monoid its place carries, composition with no axis and concatenation on one, which is why idempotence collapses one and not the other. Mass never spreads over extent: [3{4}], which a lst lays as [3,3,3,3], is a lst[int{2},int,int], while [{4}3], four ints at one slot, is not.
