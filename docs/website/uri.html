    Any obj can be referenced by a uri — from simple mono objs such as bool, int, etc. to complex nested poly objs such as rec, lst, etc. Poly objs provide another layer of addressing in that once a poly obj has a uri reference, the internal objs that make up the poly inherit that reference and extend it with any internal references necessary to find the objs within the poly. This inter/intra-poly sub-reference resolution is called a cascading resolution.

    The router is the space resolver responsible for identifying the space containing the referenced obj data.

    The space is the obj resolver responsible for identifying the obj containing the referenced data.

    The obj is the data resolver responsible for identifying the data being referenced.

    When the uri references beyond the boundaries of the obj (polys), the extended address resolution is handled through inter- and intra-poly traversals called cascades.

    Spatial Embeddings

    The Uri Reference Ring

    When a nested poly structure is written to space an ambiguity emerges. This ambiguity touches at the heart of ring theory — the relationship between \(+\) and \(*\) — and provides just enough "sleight of hand wiggle room" for useful consequences to emerge in distributed computing.

    1. the uri ring defines multiplication as "extension", where \(x \cdot y\) is x/y.

    2. the uri ring defines addition as "branching", where \(x + y\) is {x,y}.

    The characteristic marks of \(\ast\) are .,=>,<=.

    The two examples are referentially equivalent because there exists a bijection that maps A to B and B to A. In other words, they are iso-referential structures within the category of the obj ring. The form A is captured by the equation:

    \[ a \cdot (b + c) = \{1,2\} \]

    Form B has the equation:

    \[ (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c) = \{1,2\} \]

    Thus, due to the \(\small{+/*}\)-coupling axiom of ring theory which states that multiplication distributes over addition, the two equations, while typographically different, are, in fact, algebraically equivalent. Thus, the two structures, while structurally different, are referentially equivalent.

    \[ \begin{align} a \cdot (b + c) &= (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c) \\ a (b + c) &= ab + ac \\ \end{align} \]

    If the implication of these equalities is not readily apparent, then understand the following. The multiplicative union of uris to an obj is a dependent relationship. There exists a colocational requirement that a couples b and c into a single structure.

      ┌──b──⮞1

This means that a/b can be stored in a document database in Japan and a/c can be stored in a .txt file on some file system in Ohio and when resolved, the same {1,2} result is returned upon query (albeit with different space/time characteristics compared to the multiplicative, colocated representation of the data).

    Referential Factoring

    If references form a ring, then references can be factored. Sub-uri decompositions maintain referential integrity. Assume that the additive branches of the running example thus far now end with a d entry. It stands that the following equalities hold.

    a──b──d──⮞1          ┌──b──d──⮞1          ┌──b──┐                  a──b──┐
               =   a─┤              =   a─┤      ├─d──⮞{1,2}   =          ├─d──⮞{1,2}
a──c──d──⮞2          └──c──d──⮞2          └──c──┘                  a──c──┘

    The symbolic notation below reiterates what is stated diagrammatically above.

    \[ \begin{align} \; & (a \cdot b \cdot d) & + \;\; & (a \cdot c \cdot d) &=& \; \{1,2\} \\ a \cdot \; & ( (b \cdot d) & + \;\; & (c \cdot d) ) &=& \; \{1,2\} \\ a \cdot \; & ( b & + \;\; & c ) \cdot \; d &=& \; \{1,2\} \\ ( & (a \cdot b) & + \;\; & (a \cdot c)) \cdot \; d &=& \; \{1,2\} \end{align} \]
    rings of rings

    Why the unordered set \(\{1,2\}\) and not \(1+2=3\)?

    \[ \begin{align} +_\text{uri}: (\text{uri} \times \text{uri}) \rightarrow \text{uri} \\ \cdot_\text{uri}:(\text{uri} \times \text{uri}) \rightarrow \text{uri} \end{align} \]

    is not

    \[ \begin{align} +_\text{int}: (\text{int} \times \text{int}) \rightarrow \text{uri} \\ \cdot_\text{int}:(\text{int} \times \text{int}) \rightarrow \text{uri}. \end{align} \]

    These are two different + and * operators (binary functions). The + and * operators that unify the uri ring and the int ring is + and * of the obj ring. In the obj ring, 1 + 2 = {1,2} and 1 * 2 = (1 => 2).

    A subsequent, practical entailment states that all ring-based addressing schemes can be transformed from a concatenative/multiplicative form to a union/additive form so as to maintain one subset of the data in a specialized space optimized for its production requirements and the other subset can be contained in another space specialized accordingly to its requirements. For example, some data can be "hot in cache" in order to satisfy demand, while some other data can be "archivally iced" when the production usage doesn’t require a rapid response time and production costs can be reduced with cheaper disk storage. The fundamental mathematics and the subsequent algorithmic embodiment in metatron enables the symbolic manipulation of referentially consistent structural partitions. Ring-theoretic references make this possible.

    The Type-Level Ring: [] and {}

    The reference ring is usually stated over values — [a⇒{b,c}] against its {…​}-branched form — but the same two operators are the components of the type itself, carried in the uri/tid:

    • [] — the list — is · (extension). A list extends by position: lst[{2}str] is two str slots addressed as …/0, …/1, which is exactly \(x \cdot y = x/y\).

    • {} — the stream — is + (branching). A stream branches: str{3} is three `str`s, which is exactly \(x + y = \{x,y\}\).

    A type such as lst[{2}str{3}]::T{2} is therefore a term in the uri ring:

    lst[{2}str{3}]::T{2}   ≡   ((str+str+str) · (str+str+str))  +  ((str+str+str) · (str+str+str))

    where · gives the rank (how many slots extend) and ` gives the _extent_ (how many branches), alternating through the nesting. The value `[{'a','b','c'},{'d','e','f'}]` is an instance of that term and obeys the same laws: the outer `[]` extends (rank), the inner `{}` branches (extent). `{}` never nests because addition flattens — latexmath:[a(b+c) = a+b+c] — while `[] nests because multiplication extends. Hence the single invariant: no {{ }} — a stream binds to a list slot, never to another stream.

    The point is that [] and {} are not merely nominal markers on a type: they are the value-level · and + lifted to the type level. A type and its instances obey the identical algebra, so distributivity \(a \cdot (b+c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c\) holds for both.

    The above example demonstrates what is possible with referentially consistent structural embeddings. The platonic matrix \(m\) was embedded in a space using 3 different representations: entry-wise, row-wise, and row-column wise. Next, each embedding was read: an element read, a row read and a column read. The expression used to read from each of the three embeddings is the same (typographically verbatim) and so is the result (algebraically isomorphic).

    Why does the same read return the same result from all three embeddings? Because each embedding is a different factorization of the same ring term. In the uri ring, · is extension and + is branching, with distributivity \(a \cdot (b+c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c\):

    • entry-wise is the fully branched form — every element is a separate + leaf: \(m/0/0 + m/0/1 + \ldots\).

    • row-wise factors the row · out of each row’s +: \(m/0 \cdot (0+1+2+3)\).

    • row-column-wise factors the whole matrix into one · tree: \(m \cdot (\,(0 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3) + (4 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdot 7) + \ldots\,)\).

    Distributivity moves · past ` (or vice versa), so the three embeddings are the *same term* rearranged — hence `*m/0/ (a row) or m/+/0 (a column) resolves to the same value in all three. The time/space tables differ only because each factorization chooses a different *physical layout; the logical term — and so every reference into it — is unchanged.

    Navigating the Reference Structure

    Metatron is address-first: a answers the address, a answers the *value. The uri is the reference; the obj is the referent — and the same operator walks either side of the reference. The address space is first-class, obj-free navigation; the referent is what you fetch when you choose.

    mtron> n/m/r -> 1                        [-- seed a leaf --]
==>1
mtron> n/m/r/s -> 2                      [-- seed a deeper leaf --]
==>2
mtron> *n/m     ​1​
==>[r=>[s=>2]]
mtron> n/m >> r     ​2​
==>n/m/r
mtron> n/m/r <<     ​3​
==>n/m
mtron> n/m/r/s << r/s     ​4​
==>n/m
mtron> n/m/r >> 0     ​5​
==>n/m/r

    1. the branch: the child uris of n/m, each with its subtree.

    2. the walk: descend to the child r through the address tree.

    3. retract one level: the parent uri.

    4. retract a matching postfix.

    5. descend zero — the uri itself (the identity).

    >> on a poly selects a branch — keyed on a rec, indexed on a lst (the referent walk). >> on a uri extends — descends the address tree (the reference walk, nothing resolves). << is pure uri arithmetic — the uri carries its own parent in its segments, never an obj parent link. The @ anchor collapses the bridge: 5@a is the value with its address, so writes flow back (pass-by-value@reference).

    Navigation never invents an address: uri >> <path> returns the child only if it exists (it resolves through the space); otherwise noobj. Walking the reference is safe because the reference is real — a made-up path is not an address, it is a lie. See docs/design/ring-uri.md for the full theory.
