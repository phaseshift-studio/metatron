[discrete]
== Polynomial Data Structures

The **poly** types are one of the more interesting aspects of metatron. They are used to structure data (serving as collections) and for flow control (code branching). There are 3 **poly** types in metatron: `rec` (record), `lst` (list), and `rel` (relation) -- the linchpins of metatron.

[discrete]
=== data structures

The obvious application of a `poly` is as the scaffolding for a data structure. A `poly` is a https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collection_(abstract_data_type)[collection] able to bundle multiple objs
together. Via the colocation of two objs within the confines of a `poly`, two objs are bound. Another way to bind two objs together is via explicit uri referencing. This method is a form of https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazy_evaluation[lazy binding] in which "colocation" is computed through dereferencing, and as such,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangling_pointer[dangling pointers] are possible. With `poly`, there can be no "dangling pointers" -- either two objs are colocated in the `poly` or not. However, mtron is lenient on lazy pointers. It is important to realize the ramifications of the fail-free aspect of `noobj` resolved pointers.

[discrete,.center]
==== graceful degradation in the void with noobj
The intended use of metatron is to connect disparate datasets and processors into a cohesive structure bound by loose structural similarities. From resources on the web, to LLMs on networked GPU, to vertices linked within a graph database, **data entropy** will come to fragment structural bonds as different stakeholders within the larger data infrastructure independently alter their sphere of "data influence." In uncertain data environments, `noobj` and it's manifestation in maybe/maybe_some coefficients `{?}`/`{*}` make dangling pointers and type assumptions convenient for the user.

[mtron]
----
1-<[?>0,?>2,*/usr/${_}]                         [-- <1> --]
1-<[?>0,?>2,*/usr/${_}]>-                       [-- <2> --]
1-<[?>0,?>2,*/usr/${_}]>-.map(<${g:V/_}>)>>+    [-- <3> --]
----
<1> dangling pointers that lead to nowhere, lead to `noobj`.
<2> mtron silently ignores `noobj` and continues with those objs it knows.
<3> address wildcards (`+`) are a foothold in a `noobj`-world.

==== Relations

[mtron]
----
a=>b=>c       [-- <1> --]
[a=>{b,c}, /
 b=>{a,c}, /
 c=>{a,b}]    [-- <2> --]
[a,b,c]       [-- <3> --]
----
<1> a `rel` combines two objs, but due to associative nature of the `\=>` operator, a `rel` can bind an obj to another `rel`.
<2> a `rec` can be understood as a "bag of `rels`", but this conceptualization looses its hold when considering control and query.
<3> a `lst` combines objs via positional association.

[discrete,.center]
==== rel/traverse vs. to/from
Consider the following expressions:

[mtron]
----
a=>b=>c       [-- <1> --]
a->b->c       [-- <2> --]
----
<1> a `rel` associates `a` to `b\=>c` and another `rel` associates `b` to `c`.
<2> `to?uri\<=uri` associates `a` to `b` and another `to?uri\<=uri` associates `b` to `c`.

[mtron]
----
a=>b=>c.>>       [-- <1> --]
a=>b=>c.>>.>>    [-- <2> --]
a->b->c.*a       [-- <3> --]
a->b->c.**a      [-- <4> --]
----
<1> `>>?#\<=rel` maps a `rel` to it's `rng`. 
<2> Two `>>` yields the tail of the relational chain, `c`.
<3> `*?#\<=uri` maps a `uri` to an obj. 
<4> Two `**` yields the tail of the referential chain, `c`.

The third expression constructs and traverses its data structure on the stack.
The fourth expression constructs and traverses its data structure in space. 
It just so happens that the space that the fourth expression is operating in is the stack.
This is due to the fact that `<+/#>` is the stack's pattern, and therefore, matches `a`, `b`, `c`.

[mtron]
----
[== a->(b->c) ==]      [-- <1> --]
*a
**a
----
<1> `a` references the instruction `b->c`.
<2> `*a` resolves to the instruction `b->c` which, as an auto (`!`), immediately applies `a` to `b` yielding `c`.
